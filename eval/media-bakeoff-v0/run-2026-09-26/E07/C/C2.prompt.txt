B. CLAIMS FROM OUR BOOKS PULLED FOR THIS AD:
[sk_ogx_0040] the first frame decides whether the rest is seen and singularity is what sticks (ogilvy-beyond-ch2) — With thirty seconds available, the source treats the first frame as the gate: grabbing attention there with a visual surprise gives a better chance of holding the viewer, and commercials are screened out because they open with something dull. He states the asymmetry that makes this a real failure — the maker knows great things are coming and the viewer does not, and will have left before finding out. His instruction is literal: when advertising fire extinguishers, open with the fire. Alongside this he says visual banality fails, since a viewer exposed to thirty thousand commercials a year lets most slide off memory, so a commercial needs a touch of singularity, a visual burr that sticks; and he reports that commercials with a plethora of short scenes are on average below average at changing brand preference, conceding that one colleague handles many scenes without confusing people and that he himself cannot.
[sk_abcd_0007] open in the middle of the action or on a close up (google-abcd-video-ads) — The source names two concrete opening constructions for an ad — beginning in the middle of the action, and opening on a close-up — and offers bold imagery at the start as a third means. It presents them as examples of many possible ways to earn engagement immediately, not as a required opening.
[sk_abcd_0011] brand shows up early and throughout (google-abcd-video-ads) — The brand or the product is to be introduced from the start of the ad and its presence maintained across the ad's whole length. The source treats these as one instruction: an early appearance that then lapses does not satisfy it.
[sk_ogx_0039] brand attribution fails by default and must be forced by name and package (ogilvy-beyond-ch2) — The default outcome, according to the source, is that a viewer remembers the commercial and forgets which product it was for, often attributing it to a competing brand. Registering the brand is therefore treated as work that must be done deliberately rather than as a by-product of a good commercial. His stated devices are to use the name within the first ten seconds and to play games with it, such as spelling it out; he reports a commercial that repeated the brand name twenty times without irritating anyone. Commercials that end by showing the package are reported more effective at changing brand preference than those that do not. He notes many copywriters think it crass to belabour the name.
[sk_ctg_0013] remarkability is produced by violating a category expectation or by leaving a question open (berger-contagious) — The source gives two constructive routes to remarkability rather than treating it as a property a thing either has or lacks. The first is breaking an expectation the audience already holds about the category — the surprise is manufactured out of the norm, so any category with a strong norm can supply one. The second is unresolved mystery or controversy, which produces discussion because people consult others to settle the question, and disagreement generates further discussion.
[sk_sa8_0007] an eccentric picture overshadows the headline and costs the confidence it buys attention with (hopkins-scientific-advertising-ch8-21) — An eccentric or unique picture damages an advertisement in two separate ways. First it competes with the headline for the reader's attention, and since the main appeal lies in the headline, overshadowing it kills the appeal. Second it forfeits respect: treating the subject lightly, or attempting frivolity, lowers regard for the advertiser and the article, because people do not patronise a clown. The general attention gained is described as useless attention bought at the price of the attention actually wanted, and the buyers it does attract are said to be a small percentage who are not usually good buyers.
[sk_abcd_0009] bright high contrast visuals optimised for all devices (google-abcd-video-ads) — Visuals are to be kept bright and high-contrast, and the stated reason is device coverage: the ad must hold up across every device it may be watched on, which the source's own example reduces to small screens.
[sk_fre_c003_0032] refusing the eye a resting place makes the image energetic (freeman-photographers-eye-graphic-guide) — One of composition's prime jobs is keeping the interest going, so there are times when it is better to go against the expected practice of focusing attention clearly on one subject. Dividing the frame so the eye cannot rest anywhere for long produces deliberate disruption, and that makes the image more energetic. In the source's case the underlying division is still rectilinear, but set at an angle, with four faces of different sizes each in a squared-off segment keying the composition.
[sk_ms_c003_0002] camera movement must be motivated and stillness must be chosen (kenworthy-master-shots-ch8) — The book's governing rule, stated in the introduction and applied throughout: a camera move must have a reason, and so must the absence of one. The source names two opposite failures — moving because the equipment allows it, or because a wobbling camera is thought to look exciting, and leaving the camera on a tripod out of laziness — and treats both as the same error of not having decided.
[sk_gos_c003_0006] the film world must obey constant physical rules (grammar-of-the-shot-ch4) — The audience assumes a larger film world beyond the four edges of the frame, and filmmakers are obliged to present that world as conforming to constant physical rules of direction and distance so the audience is not spatially confused.
