B. CLAIMS FROM OUR BOOKS PULLED FOR THIS AD:
[sk_lsmx_0039] glass is lit at its edges not at its face (light-science-magic-beyond-ch3) — Glass shares metal's optics — almost all of its reflection is direct — and differs in that its direct reflection is often polarized. The source expects a reader to conclude that glass technique is metal technique plus a polarizer, and says explicitly that it is not. With metal the concern is the surfaces facing the camera; with glass it is the edges, and if the edges are clearly defined the front surface can often be ignored altogether. The failure the chapter exists to prevent follows from transparency: from most angles light striking a visible glass edge does not reflect toward the viewer, so the edge is invisible, and an invisible glass has no shape or form. The few reflections that do appear are usually too small and too bright to say anything about surface or texture, so they distract without informing.
[sk_lsmx_0041] dark field lighting puts one large source at the limits and a smaller dark ground inside it (light-science-magic-beyond-ch3) — Dark-field lighting inverts the same geometry: light lines against a dark background. If no light at the family's limits keeps the edge dark, light from exactly those limits makes it bright, and the glass must see light nowhere else. The plan-view diagram appears to require light at two points, but that is an artefact of drawing in two dimensions — in three dimensions the same reasoning demands a source above and behind for the rim and, on a stemmed glass, another for the underside of the bowl, which is four large sources for one small glass. The source replaces all of them with a single source large enough to cover top, bottom and sides; its exact size is not critical and any size between 10 and 25 times the subject's diameter works. A dark background, smaller than the source, sits inside it — taped to a translucent panel, or hung separately in front of a brightly lit opaque wall so the wall can be lit as much as one likes without lighting the background the camera sees.
[sk_lsmx_0045] a large dim highlight describes a glass surface where a small bright one only distracts (light-science-magic-beyond-ch3) — Defining the edges is not always enough; often the surface must be shown too, and that requires managing the highlights. Large highlights are essential, and the difference from small ones is not of degree but of function: a tiny bright spot is distracting at best and meaningless at worst, while a large highlight tells the viewer how the surface looks and feels. Producing one means filling the relevant family of angles with light, and because a rounded glass reflects much of the studio, that family can be surprisingly large. The distance from the lamp head to the diffusion sheet then becomes a design decision: close in, only the centre of the sheet is bright and the highlight is small and bright; pulled back, the whole sheet lights and reflects as a larger highlight, which the source says should usually be kept dimmer, because a brightly lit whole sheet reflects as a hard-edged rectangle that advertises a studio light and costs the scene its reality. Strips of black tape on the sheet can make the reflection read as a window instead.
[sk_lsmx_0048] a liquid filled container is a lens with its own wider field of view and its own colour problem (light-science-magic-beyond-ch3) — A round transparent container filled with liquid is a lens, and this produces two distinct problems. The first is field of view: the liquid lens sees more than the camera does, so a background that exactly fills the camera's frame does not fill the area seen through the liquid, and the studio appears as a dark surround inside the glass. The obvious remedy — a larger background — is barred, because it would sacrifice the edge definition the chapter exists to protect. The source's remedy is to move the camera closer and, if needed, fit a shorter lens to hold the image size, so the existing background fills the area seen through the liquid; the stated cost is increased perspective distortion, visible as a deeper ellipse at the rim, which may or may not be acceptable. The second problem is colour: a liquid in a transparent container always takes on some of the colour and texture of whatever is behind it, so amber beer against a mottled mid-toned ground records dark and murky. The remedy is a secondary white or silver card behind the glass, cut to the subject's own outline and lit separately, made by replacing the camera with a test light, tracing the cast shadow on the card, cutting it out and repositioning it.
[sk_lsmx_0049] the label and the glass want the same light and only a precisely sized gobo serves both (light-science-magic-beyond-ch3) — A paper label on a glass bottle is the source's worked case of two surfaces demanding incompatible treatment from one light. Most of a paper's reflection is diffuse but some is direct, so the light that gives the glass its describing highlight also puts direct reflection on the label and reduces its legibility. Two remedies work. Raise the offending light, so the label's direct reflection goes downward rather than toward the lens. Or, where good highlight placement forbids moving the light, interpose a small opaque card blocking exactly the angles that produce direct reflection on the label — and the size of that gobo is critical, because if it extends beyond the label's own family of angles it will itself reflect in the glass. A polarizer is a third option and rarely works, because much of the highlight the glass needs is usually polarized too, so removing the label's reflection interferes with the light the glass requires. Underneath all of it sits a prior question the source says cannot be settled technically: which subject is the principal one. Two identical scenes might be lit differently depending on the caption, the client, or personal whim.
[sk_ogx_0040] the first frame decides whether the rest is seen and singularity is what sticks (ogilvy-beyond-ch2) — With thirty seconds available, the source treats the first frame as the gate: grabbing attention there with a visual surprise gives a better chance of holding the viewer, and commercials are screened out because they open with something dull. He states the asymmetry that makes this a real failure — the maker knows great things are coming and the viewer does not, and will have left before finding out. His instruction is literal: when advertising fire extinguishers, open with the fire. Alongside this he says visual banality fails, since a viewer exposed to thirty thousand commercials a year lets most slide off memory, so a commercial needs a touch of singularity, a visual burr that sticks; and he reports that commercials with a plethora of short scenes are on average below average at changing brand preference, conceding that one colleague handles many scenes without confusing people and that he himself cannot.
[sk_ogx_0039] brand attribution fails by default and must be forced by name and package (ogilvy-beyond-ch2) — The default outcome, according to the source, is that a viewer remembers the commercial and forgets which product it was for, often attributing it to a competing brand. Registering the brand is therefore treated as work that must be done deliberately rather than as a by-product of a good commercial. His stated devices are to use the name within the first ten seconds and to play games with it, such as spelling it out; he reports a commercial that repeated the brand name twenty times without irritating anyone. Commercials that end by showing the package are reported more effective at changing brand preference than those that do not. He notes many copywriters think it crass to belabour the name.
[sk_abcd_0011] brand shows up early and throughout (google-abcd-video-ads) — The brand or the product is to be introduced from the start of the ad and its presence maintained across the ad's whole length. The source treats these as one instruction: an early appearance that then lapses does not satisfy it.
[sk_gos_c003_0006] the film world must obey constant physical rules (grammar-of-the-shot-ch4) — The audience assumes a larger film world beyond the four edges of the frame, and filmmakers are obliged to present that world as conforming to constant physical rules of direction and distance so the audience is not spatially confused.
[sk_ms_c003_0002] camera movement must be motivated and stillness must be chosen (kenworthy-master-shots-ch8) — The book's governing rule, stated in the introduction and applied throughout: a camera move must have a reason, and so must the absence of one.
