CUSTOMER REQUEST (verbatim — the loudest thing here): instagram feed ad (4:5) for our copper water bottle. brand Tamra, pure copper bottle 1L, ₹899. headline: Paani, jaise Dadi rakhti thi. no photos yet, make the bottle yourself.
ANSWERS: none
UNDERSTANDING: {produced at run time by B1}

MEDIA MODEL: Nano Banana 2 (image, with reference photos).  HOW THIS MODEL WANTS TO BE PROMPTED:
- **Stills** (Nano Banana 2 / GPT Image 2):
  - subject → action → setting → style, in 30–80 words;
  - state the use ("an Instagram product ad photo of …");
  - positive phrasing ("clean, unmarked surfaces"), not "no X";
  - the product comes from the reference photo: "the exact product shown in the reference image", not a long description;
  - leave a calm area where the copy will go, and name where it is.

CRAFT NOTES FROM OUR LIBRARY (use them as a sharp colleague's notes — they never override the customer's words, and
you may depart from any with a one-line reason):

A. WHAT YOU PROBABLY DON'T KNOW THAT MATTERS FOR THIS AD:
- Reflective packs: - On glass, use one large, dim highlight. - Side-light cylinders with a black card on the opposite side. - Kill label sheen by raising the light or with a gobo no larger than the label. - On metal, add black cards for the edge reflections. A dark table turns a tin's front face black, so put a light surface under it. (Light Science & Magic; lsmx_0026, lsmx_0055, lsmx_0035, lsmx_0005, lsmx_0033)
- Keep a contact shadow under cut-out products, or they float. Shoot flat products on white over a translucent, backlit base instead of trying to shade them. (Light Science & Magic; lsmx_0059, lsmx_0044)
- Position a modern product as taking over the chore while keeping the valued tradition. Where most people still make it at home, sell against home-made rather than against rival brands. (Bijapurkar, Hopkins; rbwl_0012, sa8_0021)
- Own an everyday Hindi word, set in Devanagari, in a real small-town setting, or let one familiar Indian scene make the claim with no headline. Avoid descriptors every rival already claims ("fair", "pure"). (Parameswaran, Desai; nnn_0017, nnn_0013, dmpl_0008)
- Cut defensive reassurances ("made with real…") and asterisked prices. Test each claim against "yeah, right?" (add proof) and "so what?" (tie it to a real want). Never promise above what the product delivers. (Sullivan, Hopkins; whip_0034, whip_0042, mla_0008)
- Text over a photo: - Check contrast against the least-contrasting patch of the image. - A thick outline acts as the background, so measure the letters against the outline. - Avoid hairline fonts for small supers. - Choose the brand name's typeface for legibility over mood. (WCAG, Ries; wcag_0035, wcag_0012, wcag_0033, r22_0039)
- Posters and statics: one visual shock, the promise shown in the picture, huge type, the brand readable at a distance, pure colours, and at most three elements. (Ogilvy; ogx_0015)
- Show kids choosing only kid-targeted, low-value goods; in shared household goods show their veto. Lead with the gain rather than safety or prevention unless the ad first builds the cause-and-effect link. (Parameswaran, Desai, Hopkins; nnn_0008, dmpl_0013, sa8_0017)

B. CLAIMS FROM OUR BOOKS PULLED FOR THIS AD:
[sk_lsmx_0025] polished metal is a nearly pure direct reflector so you photograph its environment (light-science-magic-beyond-ch3) — Brightly polished metal produces almost nothing but un-polarized direct reflection, and the source treats that purity as the reason metal is easy rather than hard. Because the surface behaves as a mirror, photographing metal means photographing whatever surrounds it, so a suitable environment must be prepared before the exposure. Because the behaviour is uncontaminated by other reflection types, the required light size can be worked out before any light is switched on, and a set-up that cannot work is visible early rather than after hours of trial. The source adds that this is exactly why metal is a teaching subject: it is where direct reflection can be seen behaving on its own.
[sk_lsmx_0026] bright or dark is the first metal decision and yields two opposite set ups (light-science-magic-beyond-ch3) — Before lighting metal, decide how bright it should appear. If bright, fill the family of angles that produces direct reflection; if dark, put the light anywhere else. Either way the first physical step is the same — find the family — but the two answers lead to exactly opposite arrangements, so the decision cannot be deferred. Because the family depends on where the camera is, a lighting diagram for metal is meaningless without the camera in it: the camera-to-subject relationship is at least as important as the subject.
[sk_lsmx_0037] round metal reflects the whole environment and the cameras reflection is irreducible (light-science-magic-beyond-ch3) — A round metal subject's family of angles is practically the whole environment, including the camera, and the camera's reflection always falls at the centre of the subject where it is most noticeable. There are no view-camera tricks to move the camera out of that family. Three remedies exist: camouflage, meaning any desirable clutter — an irregular surface, or additional subjects appropriate to the scene — that breaks up the unwanted reflection; keeping the camera in the dark, by confining the light to the subject, working at a distance with a long lens, or covering the camera with black material, which only works in a room large enough that the walls do not reflect; and a tent, a white enclosure that is both environment and source, with the camera outside shooting through a hole. The source is honest that the tent does not solve the problem — the dark camera hole and the tent seams reflect in the subject and must be retouched — and that it mainly makes retouching easier. It also kills the intuitive fix of building a bigger tent: moving the camera farther away shrinks its reflection but also shrinks the subject, so a longer lens is needed, which enlarges the camera's reflection back to its original size. The camera is the one reflection whose size distance cannot reduce.
[sk_lsmx_0022] cylinders need more built in tonal variation than boxes (light-science-magic-beyond-ch3) — A cylinder has no edges at which tone changes abruptly, so under even lighting the shadow blends into the highlight gradually and the subject reads as flat. Cylinders therefore need a brighter highlight side or a darker shadow side than boxes do. The source's method is a light on one side and a black card on the other. Doing so reintroduces the side-lighting shadow problem, so it also calls for a large source to keep that shadow soft enough not to compete.
[sk_ogx_0022] omitting the price loses the reader and the effect is scoped by category (ogilvy-beyond-ch2) — The price should be included wherever possible, because when it is left out people turn the page. The source's analogy is the necklace in a jeweller's window that goes unconsidered because the price is not shown and the passer-by is too shy to ask. He then scopes the claim himself: it does not matter much for packaged goods and matters a lot for products that cost real money, such as cars and refrigerators. He notes that manufacturers usually cannot state prices because they cannot dictate them to retailers, and says this reduces the selling power of their advertisements.
[sk_whip_0035] reassurance signals are read as evidence against the claim they defend (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Sullivan identifies a family of signals whose presence is itself the tell. A package promising "made with real cheese" or "contains real fruit" invites the inference that something is not real, and he gives the reductio: nobody sells a phone by saying it is made with real phone. An asterisk on a price, he argues, can legitimately be read as the sentence saying "we're lying", because it announces that a briefcase of qualifications is propping the number up. Stock photography is his third case — the manufactured handshake, the executive staring thoughtfully into a screen — which he says reeks of inauthenticity, and he names the most-demanded stock image in his own count of the genre. His stated underlying principle, taken from Frank Rose, is that authenticity is scarce and value tracks scarcity.
[sk_nnn_0017] product attribute argued entirely by a photographed situation (parameswaran-nawabs-nudes-noodles) — The Fevicol execution reproduced here carries no headline and no body copy. It is a photograph of an overcrowded Indian suburban train with dozens of men hanging off the side of a carriage, on which a Fevicol panel reading "FEVICOL — The Ultimate Adhesive" is mounted. The adhesion claim is made entirely by the photographed situation and its juxtaposition with the panel; there is no verbal claim on the page.
[sk_whip_0046] an established image carries accumulated meaning that can be borrowed but must be re angled (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Sullivan argues that certain images have acquired fixed associations — red means anger, a dog means loyal, a hand coming out of water means help — and that the trade calls this accumulated meaning the image's equity. Because a metaphor says with one image what would otherwise take twenty words, it does a great deal of work quickly and simply, and he says it is particularly valuable where the product is intangible, since a metaphor makes it tangible. He then states the condition that separates use from failure: picking up a ready-made image and setting it beside the client's logo will not work; the established image must be given a spin and used in a new and unexpected way that relates to the product advantage. He notes verbal metaphors work equally well.
[sk_sa8_0013] prevention is a weak appeal and a narrow claim can destroy a broad one (hopkins-scientific-advertising-ch8-21) — Two connected findings about which claim to feature. First, prevention is a weak appeal regardless of its merit: people will do much to cure a trouble and little to prevent one, which the source says has been proved by many disappointments — a tooth paste that prevents decay and also beautifies should be advertised on beauty, and the most successful tooth paste advertiser is said never to feature tooth troubles in headlines because tests proved them unappealing. Second, and separately, a narrow claim does not merely underperform a broad one; the source states that to even mention the eczema claim might destroy the beauty claim for a soap that does both.
[sk_hop_sa_0053] being first to state an ordinary process fact claims it (hopkins-scientific-advertising-ch1-7) — Stating an ordinary, universally shared attribute in specific detail claims it for the advertiser who states it first. The source reports that in an era when all beers were advertised as "Pure" — a claim that made no impression, and the bigger the type the bigger the folly — one brewer instead described a plate-glass room where the beer was cooled in filtered air, a white-wood-pulp filter every drop passed through, bottles washed four times by machinery, a 4,000-foot well for pure water, 1,018 experiments to obtain the yeast and the single mother cell all yeast came from. All of these were mere essentials of ordinary brewing that any brewer could have claimed, but he was first to tell people about them, and the source says he made the greatest success ever made in beer advertising. It gives a second case: "Used the world over" against "Used by the peoples of 52 nations", which many others then followed.
