A. WHAT YOU PROBABLY DON'T KNOW THAT MATTERS FOR THIS AD:
- Before writing, fix who the ad is for and what change it seeks, and restate a solution-brief as a problem with conflict. One ad covers one specific need, so split full-range briefs. (Godin, Sullivan; god_0007, whip_0010, whip_0072)
- The hook does the selling: it calls out the buyer and carries the brand and promise. If it needs explaining, it fails. No exclamation marks. (Hopkins, Ogilvy, Sullivan, Carroll; mla_0073, ogx_0001, whip_0037, whip_0038, crl_0030)
- Cut defensive reassurances ("made with real…") and asterisked prices. Test each claim against "yeah, right?" (add proof) and "so what?" (tie it to a real want). Never promise above what the product delivers. (Sullivan, Hopkins; whip_0034, whip_0042, mla_0008)
- Drop striking images that upstage the claim, and drop obvious clichés (Taj, Eiffel). The idea should fit in one sentence, and the room's enthusiasm is not proof it is big. (Hopkins, Airey, Sullivan; sa8_0009, logo_0009, logo_0016, whip_0008)
- Keep a contact shadow under cut-out products, or they float. Shoot flat products on white over a translucent, backlit base instead of trying to shade them. (Light Science & Magic; lsmx_0059, lsmx_0044)
- Cut the film to its objective: - Awareness: open with sound, show the brand early and throughout, and put people at the centre. - Conversion: make the product the hero and set the context before the ask. - Full funnel: escalate the CTAs as the film runs. Set the key sung or spoken word on screen so the film works with the sound off. (Google ABCD, Parameswaran; abcd_0010, abcd_0008, abcd_0025, abcd_0011, abcd_0012, nnn_0019)
- Text over a photo: - Check contrast against the least-contrasting patch of the image. - A thick outline acts as the background, so measure the letters against the outline. - Avoid hairline fonts for small supers. - Choose the brand name's typeface for legibility over mood. (WCAG, Ries; wcag_0035, wcag_0012, wcag_0033, r22_0039)

B. CLAIMS FROM OUR BOOKS PULLED FOR THIS AD:
[sk_whip_0046] an established image carries accumulated meaning that can be borrowed but must be re angled (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Sullivan argues that certain images have acquired fixed associations — red means anger, a dog means loyal, a hand coming out of water means help — and that the trade calls this accumulated meaning the image's equity. Because a metaphor says with one image what would otherwise take twenty words, it does a great deal of work quickly and simply, and he says it is particularly valuable where the product is intangible, since a metaphor makes it tangible. He then states the condition that separates use from failure: picking up a ready-made image and setting it beside the client's logo will not work; the established image must be given a spin and used in a new and unexpected way that relates to the product advantage. He notes verbal metaphors work equally well.
[sk_whip_0031] exaggeration is the default technique and is usually reached for before thinking (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Sullivan places exaggeration deliberately last on his list of concepting techniques and explains why: it is used so commonly that it is the first technique juniors apply, and its first hundred outputs are predictable inversions of the product benefit. He does not forbid it — he says great ideas do use it — but requires the maker to notice when they are using it. He adds Pete Barry's condition: an exaggeration must be based on a truth, or what remains is a silly contrivance, and quotes Teressa Iezzi's name for the degenerate form, the advertisement in which someone is so distracted by the product that they fail to notice some outrageous event.
[sk_ogx_0039] brand attribution fails by default and must be forced by name and package (ogilvy-beyond-ch2) — The default outcome, according to the source, is that a viewer remembers the commercial and forgets which product it was for, often attributing it to a competing brand. Registering the brand is therefore treated as work that must be done deliberately rather than as a by-product of a good commercial. His stated devices are to use the name within the first ten seconds and to play games with it, such as spelling it out; he reports a commercial that repeated the brand name twenty times without irritating anyone. Commercials that end by showing the package are reported more effective at changing brand preference than those that do not. He notes many copywriters think it crass to belabour the name.
[sk_whip_0058] humour is subordinate to interest and is not always the right register (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Sullivan subordinates humour explicitly. Funny is a subset of interesting; it is not a language but an accent; and it may not even be the right accent for a given client. His stated ordering is that funny, serious and heartfelt are all irrelevant unless the work is interesting first. Elsewhere he supplies the constraint that decides the register: the emotional choice is always a combination of what the product is and who is being addressed, and he gives the negative case — slapstick does not belong on a hospital's website.
[sk_whip_0047] demonstration outperforms assertion and can remove the need for a headline (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Sullivan states that telling people something is never as powerful as showing them, and draws a consequence about layout rather than only about persuasion: because the demonstration advertisement shows rather than tells, it does not need a clever headline. His strongest example inverts the usual relation between an advertisement and its medium — a magazine's own cover photograph, made with the advertised product, presented on the back cover with a line stating only how it was taken. He gives a second case where what is shown is the cause and the reader supplies the consequence: the mental image an anti-slip skate advertisement paints of children landing on their backsides, which he says is more powerful than showing them that way.
[sk_ctg_0053] the message must be structurally inseparable from the narrative (berger-contagious) — The source's constructive rule, and the sharpest usable test in the book: the thing you want transmitted must be load-bearing in the story, such that removing it makes the story unretellable or unfunny. He applies the test destructively to his own favourite example — the Egyptian Panda cheese advertisements would be exactly as funny with a chicken suit and a used-car tagline, which shows the humour is not what does the work — and then shows the brand passes anyway, because the story cannot be told or understood without the panda. He applies it to Blendtec: it is impossible to tell the story of the clip that destroys an iPhone without saying that a blender did it, and without conveying that the blender is extraordinarily strong, which is exactly what the company wanted communicated.
[sk_ogl_c003_0014] make the product the hero (ogilvy-ch2-advertising-that-sells) — The product itself should be made the hero of the advertising wherever possible. The source rejects the excuse that a product is too dull, holding that there are no dull products only dull writers, and reports never assigning a product to a writer not personally interested in it, because every bad campaign the author wrote was for a product that did not interest him.
[sk_whip_0007] wit works by making the viewer travel part of the distance (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Quoting McAlhone and Stuart, Sullivan holds that a witty idea deliberately does not travel all the way to the audience: the audience must travel somewhere between five and forty percent of the way to unlock it. Leave too much out and you mystify; put too much in and you bore. The completion is the mechanism, not a side effect — the reader's own act of resolving the idea is what makes it register and persist. Sullivan reports the same structure in jokes, citing Koestler's claim that hearing a joke compels the listener to re-create it in imagination.
[sk_abcd_0011] brand shows up early and throughout (google-abcd-video-ads) — The brand or the product is to be introduced from the start of the ad and its presence maintained across the ad's whole length. The source treats these as one instruction: an early appearance that then lapses does not satisfy it.
[sk_whip_0043] only one element may be the clever one (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Sullivan states a reciprocal rule and marks it as a rule in scare quotes. If the visual is the clever element doing most of the work of delivering the message, the headline should quietly clean up what is left to it. If the headline is the brilliant, well-crafted element that delivers the message, then the visual — if one exists at all — should be fairly straightforward. He shows the pair of cases run for the same client, one each way.
