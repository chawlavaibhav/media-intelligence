```
ANSWERS: none
UNDERSTANDING: {produced at run time by B1}

MEDIA MODEL: Veo 3.1 (8-second scenes with native audio; first frames by Nano Banana 2).  HOW THIS MODEL WANTS TO BE PROMPTED:
- **Veo 3.1:**
  - one 8-second scene per prompt: cinematography → subject → one action from start to end → setting → style and ambience;
  - the scene's own sound (ambience, effects), with spoken words in quotes, at most one short line;
  - keep it literal, because Veo rewrites prompts;
  - the same character description, word for word, in every scene;
  - image-to-video from a still: describe **motion and camera only**, and don't re-describe the image.

CRAFT NOTES FROM OUR LIBRARY (use them as a sharp colleague's notes — they never override the customer's words, and
you may depart from any with a one-line reason):

A. WHAT YOU PROBABLY DON'T KNOW THAT MATTERS FOR THIS AD:
- Before writing, fix who the ad is for and what change it seeks, and restate a solution-brief as a problem with conflict. One ad covers one specific need, so split full-range briefs. (Godin, Sullivan; god_0007, whip_0010, whip_0072)
- The hook does the selling: it calls out the buyer and carries the brand and promise. If it needs explaining, it fails. No exclamation marks. (Hopkins, Ogilvy, Sullivan, Carroll; mla_0073, ogx_0001, whip_0037, whip_0038, crl_0030)
- Cut defensive reassurances ("made with real…") and asterisked prices. Test each claim against "yeah, right?" (add proof) and "so what?" (tie it to a real want). Never promise above what the product delivers. (Sullivan, Hopkins; whip_0034, whip_0042, mla_0008)
- Drop striking images that upstage the claim, and drop obvious clichés (Taj, Eiffel). The idea should fit in one sentence, and the room's enthusiasm is not proof it is big. (Hopkins, Airey, Sullivan; sa8_0009, logo_0009, logo_0016, whip_0008)
- Cut the film to its objective: - Awareness: open with sound, show the brand early and throughout, and put people at the centre. - Conversion: make the product the hero and set the context before the ask. - Full funnel: escalate the CTAs as the film runs. Set the key sung or spoken word on screen so the film works with the sound off. (Google ABCD, Parameswaran; abcd_0010, abcd_0008, abcd_0025, abcd_0011, abcd_0012, nnn_0019)
- Text over a photo: - Check contrast against the least-contrasting patch of the image. - A thick outline acts as the background, so measure the letters against the outline. - Avoid hairline fonts for small supers. - Choose the brand name's typeface for legibility over mood. (WCAG, Ries; wcag_0035, wcag_0012, wcag_0033, r22_0039)
- Own one attribute nobody else owns and stay narrow. A challenger takes the colour opposite the leader. Keep a working campaign running after the team is bored with it. (Ries, Godin; r22_0014, r22_0016, r22_0041, god_0031)

B. CLAIMS FROM OUR BOOKS PULLED FOR THIS AD:
[sk_whip_0011] an idea without a conflict has no story to run on (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Sullivan holds that a brand story requires an opposing force, and that most briefs remove it. The common brief describes life after purchase — a world with no cavities, no breakdowns, no overdrafts — which he says cuts to the end of the movie and short-circuits the structure that makes a story hold attention. His stated diagnosis of an uninspiring brief is that it states a solution rather than a problem, and he says creativity happens in response to a problem, so a brief phrased as a solution gives the maker nothing to respond to. He offers a headline test for the same point: nobody reads "Area Bank Not Robbed", not because the reader is bad but because attention tunes out an unchanged status quo.
[sk_whip_0013] dramatising the absence of the product beats dramatising its presence (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Sullivan states as a general preference that showing life without the product is usually more interesting than showing life with it, and names the strategist's term for the move: a deprivation strategy. He reports that got milk? was built entirely on this tension, each execution constructing a situation where the absence of milk is a small disaster, and he warns that briefs will repeatedly ask the maker to invert it back into the with-product form.
[sk_whip_0030] every category has an inventory of worn images that must be learned in order to be avoided (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Sullivan holds that visual clichés are category-specific rather than general, and that the working procedure is therefore to go and learn which ones your category is using — visit the competitors' sites, watch their videos, study their social posts — and then not use those. He gives examples by category: grandfathers flying kites with grandchildren in insurance, earnest bespectacled people with a screen reflected in their glasses in technology. He calls the tired visual the picture version of low-hanging fruit, and prescribes the same clearing move: get them out of your system right away.
[sk_whip_0047] demonstration outperforms assertion and can remove the need for a headline (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Sullivan states that telling people something is never as powerful as showing them, and draws a consequence about layout rather than only about persuasion: because the demonstration advertisement shows rather than tells, it does not need a clever headline. His strongest example inverts the usual relation between an advertisement and its medium — a magazine's own cover photograph, made with the advertised product, presented on the back cover with a line stating only how it was taken. He gives a second case where what is shown is the cause and the reader supplies the consequence: the mental image an anti-slip skate advertisement paints of children landing on their backsides, which he says is more powerful than showing them that way.
[sk_hea_mts_0012] concreteness is expressing an idea in actions and sensory detail (heath-made-to-stick-introduction) — The third principle answers how to make an idea clear. It requires explaining the idea in terms of human actions and sensory information. The source identifies abstraction as where business communication characteristically fails — mission statements, synergies, strategies and visions ambiguous to the point of meaninglessness — and makes an unusually strong claim for the remedy: speaking concretely is the *only* way to ensure an idea means the same thing to everyone in the audience.
[sk_hea_mts_0016] people feel for individuals and not for abstractions (heath-made-to-stick-introduction) — Feeling attaches to a person and not to a category. The source states that research shows people are more likely to give to a single named needy individual than to a whole impoverished region, and generalises it: we are wired to feel things for people, not for abstractions.
[sk_ogx_0039] brand attribution fails by default and must be forced by name and package (ogilvy-beyond-ch2) — The default outcome, according to the source, is that a viewer remembers the commercial and forgets which product it was for, often attributing it to a competing brand. Registering the brand is therefore treated as work that must be done deliberately rather than as a by-product of a good commercial. His stated devices are to use the name within the first ten seconds and to play games with it, such as spelling it out; he reports a commercial that repeated the brand name twenty times without irritating anyone. Commercials that end by showing the package are reported more effective at changing brand preference than those that do not. He notes many copywriters think it crass to belabour the name.
[sk_abcd_0006] jump in reach the heart of the story faster (google-abcd-video-ads) — The source's named technique "Jump in" asks the ad to reach the heart of its story sooner than it otherwise would, and names two specific means of doing so: pacing that keeps the viewer engaged, and tight framing.
[sk_ctg_0053] the message must be structurally inseparable from the narrative (berger-contagious) — The source's constructive rule, and the sharpest usable test in the book: the thing you want transmitted must be load-bearing in the story, such that removing it makes the story unretellable or unfunny. He applies the test destructively to his own favourite example — the Egyptian Panda cheese advertisements would be exactly as funny with a chicken suit and a used-car tagline, which shows the humour is not what does the work — and then shows the brand passes anyway, because the story cannot be told or understood without the panda. He applies it to Blendtec: it is impossible to tell the story of the clip that destroys an iPhone without saying that a blender did it, and without conveying that the blender is extraordinarily strong, which is exactly what the company wanted communicated.
[sk_whip_0060] how long the viewer dwells with the medium sets the permissible fuse length (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Sullivan treats comprehension budget as a property of the medium rather than of the idea, and varies his prescriptions accordingly. For out-of-home he reports the received limit of about seven words, notes the logo adds two or three, calls ten words pushing it, and says eleven can work with the right execution and no visual. He prescribes two checkable tests: show it to someone for two seconds, take it away and ask what it said; and view the layout from ten feet away for legibility. He then states the differential explicitly — subway postings and bus stops can risk a slightly longer fuse than a roadside board, because the audience dwells. His craft advice follows the same logic: when a campaign includes outdoor, start with the outdoor, and pretend there is no photography budget, because that forces the thinking to boil down.
```

```
Here is your draft (JSON) for this ad and the customer's request (verbatim). Answer each question YES or NO,
quoting the line of your draft that proves it. For every NO, either revise the draft or keep it with a one-line reason

a question. Return the full revised JSON plus "checklist":[{"id","answer","evidence","action"}].

C01. Can you say the idea in one sentence, and would it be wrong for any other product?
C02. Is there a real obstacle or tension before the product appears (not just life after purchase)?
C03. Is the customer the hero and the brand the guide/enabler (not the brand bragging)?
C04. Would the first 2 seconds stop a thumb — mid-action, a close-up, or a bold image?
C05. Is the brand or product seen early, again later, and through more than one kind of asset — so a viewer could not credit a competitor?
C06. Does the film make the viewer FEEL something, and is each scene's feeling stated and shown (not a feature list)?

C11. Will the payoff and the product read clearly on a phone screen (size, time on screen)?
```
