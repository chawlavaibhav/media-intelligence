```

CUSTOMER REQUEST (verbatim — the loudest thing here): launch poster for the Mokobara Transit Backpack 30L. headline: Room for the long way home. also show Transit Backpack · 30L and mokobara.com. use our photos and logo. need 1:1 and 4:5.

  - state the use ("an Instagram product ad photo of …");

  - leave a calm area where the copy will go, and name where it is.

CRAFT NOTES FROM OUR LIBRARY (use them as a sharp colleague's notes — they never override the customer's words, and
you may depart from any with a one-line reason):

A. WHAT YOU PROBABLY DON'T KNOW THAT MATTERS FOR THIS AD:
- Text over a photo: - Check contrast against the least-contrasting patch of the image. - A thick outline acts as the background, so measure the letters against the outline. - Avoid hairline fonts for small supers. - Choose the brand name's typeface for legibility over mood. (WCAG, Ries; wcag_0035, wcag_0012, wcag_0033, r22_0039)
- Posters and statics: one visual shock, the promise shown in the picture, huge type, the brand readable at a distance, pure colours, and at most three elements. (Ogilvy; ogx_0015)
- Keep a contact shadow under cut-out products, or they float. Shoot flat products on white over a translucent, backlit base instead of trying to shade them. (Light Science & Magic; lsmx_0059, lsmx_0044)
- A small, saturated subject can sit near a corner of a muted frame. Point the model's gaze at the product, freeze motion at the apex, and stack 9:16 frames up toward one peak. Pastel means brighter, not desaturated. (Freeman; pex_0041, pex_0015, pex_0045, pex_0006, pex_0018)
- Drop striking images that upstage the claim, and drop obvious clichés (Taj, Eiffel). The idea should fit in one sentence, and the room's enthusiasm is not proof it is big. (Hopkins, Airey, Sullivan; sa8_0009, logo_0009, logo_0016, whip_0008)

B. CLAIMS FROM OUR BOOKS PULLED FOR THIS AD:
[sk_ogx_0015] illustration discipline one subject life size and no history (ogilvy-beyond-ch2) — A short list of illustration constraints is stated as observed audience reactions: keep the illustration as simple as possible with the focus of interest on one person, because crowd scenes do not pull; do not enlarge human faces beyond life size, because they seem to repel readers; historical subjects bore the majority of readers. To these the source adds that characters known from a brand's television commercials boost recall of its print advertisements, and that for cooking products a photograph of the finished dish attracts more readers than one of the ingredients.
[sk_sa8_0006] pictures pay where they depict the condition the buyer covets (hopkins-scientific-advertising-ch8-21) — Where pictures do pay, the source says they pay by depicting the condition the buyer wants to reach rather than the article for sale, so that the picture carries the implication that the article delivers that condition. The cases given are apparel (men others envy, in surroundings others covet), correspondence schools (men in high positions or taking upward steps), and beauty articles (beautiful women, admired and attractive) — with the added instruction to show the beauty being used to its effect on a fascinated man, on the stated ground that women desire beauty largely because of men.
[sk_ogx_0041] show the product in use and in motion and repeat one visual device over years (ogilvy-beyond-ch2) — Showing the product in use pays, and where possible the end-result of using it — how a nappy keeps a baby dry, how pistons look after fifty thousand miles. For food the more appetising it looks the more it sells, and food in motion is said to look particularly appetising: chocolate sauce in the act of being poured, syrup over pancakes. Close-ups pay when the product is the hero of the commercial. Over a longer horizon he names mnemonics, a visual device repeated over a long period, which he says can increase brand identification and remind people of the promise.
[sk_sam_c003_0021] text over image needs value contrast and still follows the structure (samara-making-breaking-grid-ch1) — Text may be set over an image provided there is enough contrast between their relative values for the text to remain legible. The text's visual qualities must play off those of the image while still following the structure underneath.
[sk_whip_0048] the distance between word and image is the variable that sets the size of the effect (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Sullivan treats the headline-visual relationship as a quantity rather than a binary. Reporting Pete Barry, he says many concepts start with a headline that sets up a visual, the idea is then revealed by the visual, and the unexpectedness is what creates the tension between the two elements — and he states the scaling claim: the greater the polarity between headline and visual, the bigger the shift in the reader's head. He also states the relation is reversible: tension introduced by a headline can be released by the visual, or a dominant visual can be reframed in the reader's head by the headline that follows it.
[sk_ogx_0039] brand attribution fails by default and must be forced by name and package (ogilvy-beyond-ch2) — The default outcome, according to the source, is that a viewer remembers the commercial and forgets which product it was for, often attributing it to a competing brand. Registering the brand is therefore treated as work that must be done deliberately rather than as a by-product of a good commercial. His stated devices are to use the name within the first ten seconds and to play games with it, such as spelling it out; he reports a commercial that repeated the brand name twenty times without irritating anyone. Commercials that end by showing the package are reported more effective at changing brand preference than those that do not. He notes many copywriters think it crass to belabour the name.
[sk_whip_0053] one element must be designated as the entry point (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Sullivan states that whether it is a big headline, a large visual or a single word floating in white space, somebody has got to be the boss, and that every design needs an overall visual hierarchy — an implied order of the relative importance of the elements of the whole. He quotes Roy Grace for the operational version: there has to be a point on every page where the art director and writer want the reader to start, and there has to be an understanding, an agreement and a logical reason for where that is. He repeats the requirement inside the letter-or-postcard decision, which is why it functions as a precondition rather than as one recommendation among others.
[sk_fre_c003_0009] the square frame reads as strict and formal (freeman-photographers-eye-graphic-guide) — The square was never treated in the visual arts as a natural frame and was used only occasionally, often to make a point about rigour and severity. Its equal sides give a sense of strictness, formality, even constraint. Photography was different, because for decades medium-format 6x6cm film pushed photographers into finding square compositions, and they found that square could be good.
[sk_sam_c003_0079] deriving every format from one module gives cohesion and cheaper production (samara-making-breaking-grid-ch1) — Grids are especially effective for unifying complex campaigns or identity programmes, where messaging, function, complexity and medium all vary — and it is there that the tension between a grid's consistent architecture and its need to flex comes to the fore. If the designer can anticipate most of the materials to be produced, the formats of the various communications can all be outgrowths of a column or module proportion, or the proportion derived from the formats. Regulating formats across touchpoints and media yields greater visual cohesion, most importantly; it also means the formats can be used harmoniously together, and that formats produced by similar processes are more likely to be producible simultaneously and therefore more cheaply.
[sk_wcag_0022] thin or unfamiliar letterforms are harder to read especially at lower contrast (w3c-wcag22-text-legibility) — Fonts with extraordinarily thin strokes, or unusual features and characteristics that reduce the familiarity of their letterforms, are harder to read — and the source specifies that this is especially so at lower contrast levels. Stroke weight and letterform familiarity interact with contrast rather than being independent of it.
```
