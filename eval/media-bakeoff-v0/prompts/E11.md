CRAFT NOTES FROM OUR LIBRARY (use them as a sharp colleague's notes — they never override the customer's words, and
you may depart from any with a one-line reason):

A. WHAT YOU PROBABLY DON'T KNOW THAT MATTERS FOR THIS AD:
- Before writing, fix who the ad is for and what change it seeks, and restate a solution-brief as a problem with conflict. One ad covers one specific need, so split full-range briefs. (Godin, Sullivan; god_0007, whip_0010, whip_0072)
- The hook does the selling: it calls out the buyer and carries the brand and promise. If it needs explaining, it fails. No exclamation marks. (Hopkins, Ogilvy, Sullivan, Carroll; mla_0073, ogx_0001, whip_0037, whip_0038, crl_0030)
- Drop striking images that upstage the claim, and drop obvious clichés (Taj, Eiffel). The idea should fit in one sentence, and the room's enthusiasm is not proof it is big. (Hopkins, Airey, Sullivan; sa8_0009, logo_0009, logo_0016, whip_0008)
- Never publicise how many people do the bad habit you oppose; sell through "people like us do this". Anchor the product to a frequent everyday cue (chai time) at the moment the viewer can act. (Berger, Godin; ctg_0035, ctg_0036, ctg_0021, ctg_0022, ctg_0020, god_0017, god_0018)
- Give the lowest-friction response route (WhatsApp or call beats a form), add a dated inducement against delay, and name local stockists in the ad. (Hopkins; sa8_0030, sa8_0051, mla_0063)
- A small, saturated subject can sit near a corner of a muted frame. Point the model's gaze at the product, freeze motion at the apex, and stack 9:16 frames up toward one peak. Pastel means brighter, not desaturated. (Freeman; pex_0041, pex_0015, pex_0045, pex_0006, pex_0018)
- Cut the film to its objective: - Awareness: open with sound, show the brand early and throughout, and put people at the centre. - Conversion: make the product the hero and set the context before the ask. - Full funnel: escalate the CTAs as the film runs. Set the key sung or spoken word on screen so the film works with the sound off. (Google ABCD, Parameswaran; abcd_0010, abcd_0008, abcd_0025, abcd_0011, abcd_0012, nnn_0019)
- Own one attribute nobody else owns and stay narrow. A challenger takes the colour opposite the leader. Keep a working campaign running after the team is bored with it. (Ries, Godin; r22_0014, r22_0016, r22_0041, god_0031)

B. CLAIMS FROM OUR BOOKS PULLED FOR THIS AD:
[sk_ctg_0013] remarkability is produced by violating a category expectation or by leaving a question open (berger-contagious) — The source gives two constructive routes to remarkability rather than treating it as a property a thing either has or lacks. The first is breaking an expectation the audience already holds about the category — the surprise is manufactured out of the norm, so any category with a strong norm can supply one. The second is unresolved mystery or controversy, which produces discussion because people consult others to settle the question, and disagreement generates further discussion.
[sk_whip_0064] deliberate collision with a distant subject domain and naming the idea before building it (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Sullivan reports Monahan's technique of listing forty or fifty data points about the brand's own subject, then building a second list from a subject chosen to be as far from it as possible, and connecting points across the two. He passes on Monahan's selection criterion for the second domain — familiar, unrelated to your work, and rich in data points — and his named rejects, which are unrelated but thin. Sullivan then offers a variant with the opposite setting: use a nearby domain instead, drawn from the truths of the product, category or customer, and use the collision to produce the campaign's name first, then write the campaign from the name. His stated analogy is coming up with the title before writing the book. He reports Thomas Kemeny on why the name matters operationally: it is the shorthand everyone uses internally and externally, what the client repeats, and how the press introduces it, and it should be telegraphic and clear about what the idea is. He is careful to note not every campaign needs one.
[sk_whip_0004] a concept that makes more than one point makes none (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Sullivan states flatly that to create a simple advertising idea in any medium the maker must be limited to saying one thing, and that any advertising concept trying to make more than one point will not work. He extends this to the client request for a full-line advertisement: if a client has three important things to say, they need three advertisements, because customers shop for specific needs and never for a full line.
[sk_ctg_0025] a trigger must fire close in time and place to the action it is meant to cause (berger-contagious) — Frequency is not sufficient: the source adds a proximity condition. A trigger that fires where or when the person cannot act is wasted, however memorable the message. His minimal pair is a New Zealand safety advertisement that makes the viewer think about bath mats in the bathroom, where no mat can be bought, against New York City's "Man Drinks Fat" video, which fires the message about sugary drinks at the moment someone is about to have one. He draws a related consequence for measurement: the longer the delay between exposure and the desired action, the more the trigger matters, and market research that captures immediate reaction is measuring the wrong moment for most purchases.
[sk_ogx_0041] show the product in use and in motion and repeat one visual device over years (ogilvy-beyond-ch2) — Showing the product in use pays, and where possible the end-result of using it — how a nappy keeps a baby dry, how pistons look after fifty thousand miles. For food the more appetising it looks the more it sells, and food in motion is said to look particularly appetising: chocolate sauce in the act of being poured, syrup over pancakes. Close-ups pay when the product is the hero of the commercial. Over a longer horizon he names mnemonics, a visual device repeated over a long period, which he says can increase brand identification and remind people of the promise.
[sk_whip_0030] every category has an inventory of worn images that must be learned in order to be avoided (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Sullivan holds that visual clichés are category-specific rather than general, and that the working procedure is therefore to go and learn which ones your category is using — visit the competitors' sites, watch their videos, study their social posts — and then not use those. He gives examples by category: grandfathers flying kites with grandchildren in insurance, earnest bespectacled people with a screen reflected in their glasses in technology. He calls the tired visual the picture version of low-hanging fruit, and prescribes the same clearing move: get them out of your system right away.
[sk_mla_0018] attach the product to a named person and borrow that persons voice (hopkins-my-life-in-advertising) — The source claims that naming and featuring an individual is one of its most reliable devices, and that a person can be made famous far more easily than a firm. It reports three uses of it. Featuring an unknown chief engineer by name gave a mid-priced car a distinction against a better-known and handsomer rival at the same price. Making a designer sign the advertisements borrowed his existing reputation and let the copy be written to characterise him. And writing an entire selling book in the name and voice of an independent practical user — a chicken raiser who cared more for serving than for selling — produced a catalogue that read unlike the five or six identical ones the same prospect had requested. It states that a person featured this way is unknown at the start, that advertising makes him prominent, and that his name then becomes an exclusive feature of great value.
[sk_whip_0043] only one element may be the clever one (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Sullivan states a reciprocal rule and marks it as a rule in scare quotes. If the visual is the clever element doing most of the work of delivering the message, the headline should quietly clean up what is left to it. If the headline is the brilliant, well-crafted element that delivers the message, then the visual — if one exists at all — should be fairly straightforward. He shows the pair of cases run for the same client, one each way.
[sk_abcd_0007] open in the middle of the action or on a close up (google-abcd-video-ads) — The source names two concrete opening constructions for an ad — beginning in the middle of the action, and opening on a close-up — and offers bold imagery at the start as a third means. It presents them as examples of many possible ways to earn engagement immediately, not as a required opening.
[sk_ctg_0009] sharing is self presentation so people share what reflects well on them (berger-contagious) — The source's first driver is that people choose what to say partly by what saying it will make others think of them. Because choices signal identity, and because what a person talks about is read the same way as the car they drive or the clothes they wear, word of mouth functions as currency spent on desired impressions. The prediction he draws is symmetric and he states the negative half explicitly: people withhold the story that would make them look bad as reliably as they pass on the one that makes them look good.
