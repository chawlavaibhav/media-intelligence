A. WHAT YOU PROBABLY DON'T KNOW THAT MATTERS FOR THIS AD:
- Keep a contact shadow under cut-out products, or they float. Shoot flat products on white over a translucent, backlit base instead of trying to shade them. (Light Science & Magic; lsmx_0059, lsmx_0044)

B. CLAIMS FROM OUR BOOKS PULLED FOR THIS AD:
[sk_alt_c003_0026] light from one consistent direction because audiences read light without being taught (alton-painting-with-light-ch2) — The source rejects the older practice of lighting a figure at a sunlit window from two contradictory directions — window from outside, face flatly from inside — because the result feels contradictory. Its replacement lights realistically from the established direction: an arc from outside, incandescent mixed in from the same direction, a weaker crosslight inside, and a heavily diffused broad beside the camera. The justification is that audiences read light without training, so subdued low-key faces with strong cross and backlight are now accepted without comment even in daylight shots.
[sk_alt_c003_0011] establish the fictional light source first then place the key to agree with it (alton-painting-with-light-ch2) — The first act in lighting a scene is to decide where the light is supposed to be coming from — the source lists sunlight, moonlight, skyshine and the northern lights among natural sources, and street lights, headlights, lighthouses, a stage spotlight, flashlight, lantern, candle, oil burner, match, torch, fireplace, campfire and phosphorescence among artificial ones. The key light is then placed in the position that both lights the scene properly and appears to come from that source, and a small testlight is used to find it.
[sk_lsmx_0019] the ground shadow must be relocated rather than removed (light-science-magic-beyond-ch3) — A competing shadow cannot simply be softened out of existence, because the shadow is carrying information: it proves the subject is resting on a surface rather than floating above it, and the relationship of subject to background is an essential part of the depth message. The remedy is to move it, not to delete it. The least distracting position in most compositions is directly under and in front of the subject, which means the light goes above and slightly behind. Softening it afterwards with a larger source makes it unobtrusive without losing the grounding.
[sk_lsmx_0058] mixed colour is correctable and unmixed colour is not so match the sources to each other (light-science-magic-beyond-ch3) — Two situations arise when light sources of different colours are present, and only one is a problem. In mixed colour, rays of different balance blend so that the whole scene is lit by a single new balance: the error is uniform, so the camera will probably fix it and postproduction certainly can, with the result differing from a correctly shot frame only under side-by-side comparison. In unmixed colour, different areas are lit by different sources — flash on the foreground subject, fluorescent overhead, and, in a case the source flags as easy to miss, a flash-cast shadow on a wall filled in by fluorescent light and rendering yellow-green. No white balance can correct this, because whatever is right for one area is wrong for another, and a compromise value makes nothing right. The remedy is counter-intuitive: gel the sources to match each other as closely as possible, aiming for a single colour and not necessarily the correct one, then let the camera correct the whole scene globally. The source's example is a light-green gel on the flash to approximate the overhead fluorescents.
[sk_alt_c003_0015] depth comes from tonal separation between foreground and background (alton-painting-with-light-ch2) — The illusion of depth is produced by separating foreground from background in tone, in either direction — dark against light or light against dark. The source states its own summary of a complete picture as the conjunction of three things: an angle showing as many surfaces as possible, each of those surfaces at a different brightness, and a background of a different tone again.
[sk_crl_0014] shallow depth of field requires separation in depth to act on (carroll-read-this-photographs) — A shallow depth of field only produces a visible effect if the scene contains something at a different distance for it to blur. Carroll states this as a boundary condition on his own technique: without a good amount of distance behind or in front of the subject there is nothing to fall out of focus, and the wide aperture buys nothing. He adds the compounding factor that depth of field is at its shallowest in close-up, so proximity, aperture and a longer focal length stack.
[sk_crl_0016] metered exposure fails predictably on extremes and backlight (carroll-read-this-photographs) — The camera's own exposure fails in a direction that can be predicted from the scene: a very bright subject such as snow comes out grey and underexposed, a very dark scene comes out too bright, and a subject in front of a window becomes a silhouette because the interior light is much darker relative to the exterior. Because the direction of the error is predictable, the correction is a single control: exposure compensation towards + to recover shadow detail, towards − to darken. Carroll attaches an explicit priority to the + case — accept blown highlights so long as detail in the subject survives — and treats the − case as a positive technique rather than a fault, since underexposing a backlit subject is how a silhouette is made. He warns that the compensation persists and must be returned to zero.
[sk_lsmx_0016] viewpoint couples perspective distortion and the family of angles (light-science-magic-beyond-ch3) — Camera position is a single control acting on two independent-seeming outcomes. It determines perspective distortion — closer means more convergence, farther means less — and it determines the family of angles that produces direct reflection. The two therefore cannot be adjusted separately: moving the camera to escape a reflection changes the apparent geometry of the subject, and moving it to change the apparent geometry changes which lights will reflect. The source separately insists that focal length does not cause perspective distortion; it only lets a chosen viewpoint fill the frame, and the authors prove this by shooting all their comparison frames on one wide lens and enlarging.
[sk_lsmx_0015] depth in a flat image rests on two clues perspective distortion and tonal variation (light-science-magic-beyond-ch3) — A photograph is as two-dimensional as a drawing, and the depth a viewer perceives in it is manufactured by two visual clues: perspective distortion, in which edges that are equal appear unequal and right angles appear not to be right angles, and tonal variation, the presence of both light and dark areas. The source demonstrates with a line drawing of a cube that lacks both clues and is not reliably read as a cube at all, and one with both clues that is. Lighting affects both: obviously through highlight and shadow, less obviously through the coupling described separately.
[sk_alt_c003_0027] imitating nature exactly can fail and the target is the viewers mental image (alton-painting-with-light-ch2) — Two statements that qualify the chapter's realism. Simply imitating natural sunlight for a shot taken through a window from outside would burn out the wall and the window, so the light is shaded with gauze or a tree branch, the arc placed high to one side so it does not reflect in the glass, and a kicker added from inside to round the face — while backlight is omitted because it would not look natural. And when exteriors are built inside, the thing being matched is stated to be the audience's mental image of an exterior rather than the exterior itself.
