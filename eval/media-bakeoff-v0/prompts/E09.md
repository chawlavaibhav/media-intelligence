- **Veo 3.1:**
  - one 8-second scene per prompt: cinematography → subject → one action from start to end → setting → style and ambience;
  - the scene's own sound (ambience, effects), with spoken words in quotes, at most one short line;
  - keep it literal, because Veo rewrites prompts;
  - the same character description, word for word, in every scene;
  - image-to-video from a still: describe **motion and camera only**, and don't re-describe the image.

CRAFT NOTES FROM OUR LIBRARY (use them as a sharp colleague's notes — they never override the customer's words, and
you may depart from any with a one-line reason):

A. WHAT YOU PROBABLY DON'T KNOW THAT MATTERS FOR THIS AD:
- Status only exists once others register it, so show the product being noticed. With mass audiences, visible popularity is itself the appeal. (Desai, Jain; dmpl_0012, jgb_0011)
- Before writing, fix who the ad is for and what change it seeks, and restate a solution-brief as a problem with conflict. One ad covers one specific need, so split full-range briefs. (Godin, Sullivan; god_0007, whip_0010, whip_0072)
- The hook does the selling: it calls out the buyer and carries the brand and promise. If it needs explaining, it fails. No exclamation marks. (Hopkins, Ogilvy, Sullivan, Carroll; mla_0073, ogx_0001, whip_0037, whip_0038, crl_0030)
- Cut defensive reassurances ("made with real…") and asterisked prices. Test each claim against "yeah, right?" (add proof) and "so what?" (tie it to a real want). Never promise above what the product delivers. (Sullivan, Hopkins; whip_0034, whip_0042, mla_0008)
- Drop striking images that upstage the claim, and drop obvious clichés (Taj, Eiffel). The idea should fit in one sentence, and the room's enthusiasm is not proof it is big. (Hopkins, Airey, Sullivan; sa8_0009, logo_0009, logo_0016, whip_0008)
- Cast real, unpolished customers, and have the interviewer challenge the product so they defend it. Don't add a celebrity just to pass pre-tests. (Ogilvy, Parameswaran; ogx_0021, nnn_0011)
- Never publicise how many people do the bad habit you oppose; sell through "people like us do this". Anchor the product to a frequent everyday cue (chai time) at the moment the viewer can act. (Berger, Godin; ctg_0035, ctg_0036, ctg_0021, ctg_0022, ctg_0020, god_0017, god_0018)
- Cut the film to its objective: - Awareness: open with sound, show the brand early and throughout, and put people at the centre. - Conversion: make the product the hero and set the context before the ask. - Full funnel: escalate the CTAs as the film runs. Set the key sung or spoken word on screen so the film works with the sound off. (Google ABCD, Parameswaran; abcd_0010, abcd_0008, abcd_0025, abcd_0011, abcd_0012, nnn_0019)

B. CLAIMS FROM OUR BOOKS PULLED FOR THIS AD:
[sk_mla_0009] state the result the buyer wants not the construction that produces it (hopkins-my-life-in-advertising) — The source claims that manufacturers habitually advertise the construction they are proud of rather than the outcome the buyer wants, and that converting a construction fact into a result claim is often the whole campaign. It gives the Goodyear case explicitly. The company was promoting a construction called the straight-side tire, which the source had seen advertised repeatedly without ever learning what it meant; on asking, it found the construction produced two results — the tire would not rim-cut, and it held ten per cent more air size for size. Renaming the product for the results, and heading every advertisement with those results, produced the campaign. The earlier carpet-sweeper case makes the same point against broom action, dumping devices and bearings.
[sk_hea_mts_0022] the curse of knowledge is why obvious principles are not followed (heath-made-to-stick-introduction) — The reason the six principles are not already in general use, despite most of them sounding like common sense, is a single psychological tendency the source names the Curse of Knowledge: once a person knows something they find it hard to imagine not knowing it, and so cannot re-create the listener's state of mind. The source states that this is reenacted daily between CEOs and frontline employees, teachers and students, politicians and voters, marketers and customers, writers and readers — all of them separated by an information imbalance. When a chief executive discusses "unlocking shareholder value" there is a tune playing in her head that the employees cannot hear.
[sk_hea_mts_0012] concreteness is expressing an idea in actions and sensory detail (heath-made-to-stick-introduction) — The third principle answers how to make an idea clear. It requires explaining the idea in terms of human actions and sensory information. The source identifies abstraction as where business communication characteristically fails — mission statements, synergies, strategies and visions ambiguous to the point of meaninglessness — and makes an unusually strong claim for the remedy: speaking concretely is the *only* way to ensure an idea means the same thing to everyone in the audience.
[sk_whip_0004] a concept that makes more than one point makes none (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Sullivan states flatly that to create a simple advertising idea in any medium the maker must be limited to saying one thing, and that any advertising concept trying to make more than one point will not work. He extends this to the client request for a full-line advertisement: if a client has three important things to say, they need three advertisements, because customers shop for specific needs and never for a full line.
[sk_whip_0030] every category has an inventory of worn images that must be learned in order to be avoided (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Sullivan holds that visual clichés are category-specific rather than general, and that the working procedure is therefore to go and learn which ones your category is using — visit the competitors' sites, watch their videos, study their social posts — and then not use those. He gives examples by category: grandfathers flying kites with grandchildren in insurance, earnest bespectacled people with a screen reflected in their glasses in technology. He calls the tired visual the picture version of low-hanging fruit, and prescribes the same clearing move: get them out of your system right away.
[sk_whip_0047] demonstration outperforms assertion and can remove the need for a headline (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Sullivan states that telling people something is never as powerful as showing them, and draws a consequence about layout rather than only about persuasion: because the demonstration advertisement shows rather than tells, it does not need a clever headline. His strongest example inverts the usual relation between an advertisement and its medium — a magazine's own cover photograph, made with the advertised product, presented on the back cover with a line stating only how it was taken. He gives a second case where what is shown is the cause and the reader supplies the consequence: the mental image an anti-slip skate advertisement paints of children landing on their backsides, which he says is more powerful than showing them that way.
[sk_ogx_0041] show the product in use and in motion and repeat one visual device over years (ogilvy-beyond-ch2) — Showing the product in use pays, and where possible the end-result of using it — how a nappy keeps a baby dry, how pistons look after fifty thousand miles. For food the more appetising it looks the more it sells, and food in motion is said to look particularly appetising: chocolate sauce in the act of being poured, syrup over pancakes. Close-ups pay when the product is the hero of the commercial. Over a longer horizon he names mnemonics, a visual device repeated over a long period, which he says can increase brand identification and remind people of the promise.
[sk_whip_0041] a fact about the product is not the same thing as a truth about the customer (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Sullivan distinguishes facts from truths and says the distinction decides whether a campaign has anywhere to go. Facts are measurable and fit on charts, which is why clients like them, and he argues nobody cares about them: his standing example is "milk builds strong bones", a fact everyone has heard since childhood which changes no behaviour. A truth is what is undeniably and universally the case about the product, the category or above all the customer, and he reports that the campaign which sold milk by the trainload came from ethnographic observation producing a different kind of finding — that milk is bought because it goes with things. He adds two consequences: a truth is generally known instinctively rather than researched, and clients frequently pay to uncover truths and then cover them back up.
[sk_abcd_0007] open in the middle of the action or on a close up (google-abcd-video-ads) — The source names two concrete opening constructions for an ad — beginning in the middle of the action, and opening on a close-up — and offers bold imagery at the start as a third means. It presents them as examples of many possible ways to earn engagement immediately, not as a required opening.
[sk_whip_0060] how long the viewer dwells with the medium sets the permissible fuse length (sullivan-hey-whipple) — Sullivan treats comprehension budget as a property of the medium rather than of the idea, and varies his prescriptions accordingly. For out-of-home he reports the received limit of about seven words, notes the logo adds two or three, calls ten words pushing it, and says eleven can work with the right execution and no visual. He prescribes two checkable tests: show it to someone for two seconds, take it away and ask what it said; and view the layout from ten feet away for legibility. He then states the differential explicitly — subway postings and bus stops can risk a slightly longer fuse than a roadside board, because the audience dwells. His craft advice follows the same logic: when a campaign includes outdoor, start with the outdoor, and pretend there is no photography budget, because that forces the thinking to boil down.
