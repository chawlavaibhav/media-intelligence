== sk_murch_c003_0002 | murch_blink_p1_25_editing_decisions | shadow_splices_outnumber_kept_splices_about_fifteen_to_one
   Most of the cuts an editor makes are not kept. On Apocalypse Now roughly fifteen splices were made, considered and then undone or lifted for every splice that survived into the finished film.

== sk_murch_c003_0004 | murch_blink_p1_25_editing_decisions | the_cut_is_a_total_instantaneous_displacement_unlike_lived_perception
   A cut is the total and instantaneous replacement of one field of vision by another, often carrying a jump in time as well as space. Nothing in ordinary experience prepares a viewer for it: from waking to sleeping, perceived visual reality is a continuous stream of linked images, and life on Earth has experienced the world that way for hundreds of millions of years. Edited film confronted human beings with something else only at the beginning of the twentieth century.

== sk_murch_c003_0005 | murch_blink_p1_25_editing_decisions | why_cuts_work_is_posed_as_an_open_question
   That cuts work is treated by the source as an established fact and as a genuine puzzle. It states the question explicitly — why does a total displacement of the visual field not break the viewer's experience — likens it to the bumble-bee that should not be able to fly, and defers the answer.

== sk_murch_c003_0006 | murch_blink_p1_25_editing_decisions | continuous_motion_is_already_twenty_four_displacements_a_second
   A continuous shot is not continuous. Each frame is a displacement from the one before, and film is being cut twenty-four times a second. What makes a shot read as motion rather than as twenty-four separate contexts is that the frame-to-frame displacement is small — twenty milliseconds — not that it is absent.

== sk_murch_c003_0008 | murch_blink_p1_25_editing_decisions | discontinuous_shooting_freed_film_from_time_and_space
   The discovery that certain kinds of cutting worked led immediately to the discovery that films could be shot discontinuously, which the source calls the cinematic equivalent of the discovery of flight: films were no longer earthbound in time and space. Discontinuity is the central fact of the production phase, and almost all production decisions relate to overcoming its difficulties or exploiting its strengths.

== sk_murch_c003_0009 | murch_blink_p1_25_editing_decisions | discontinuity_permits_selecting_the_best_angle_for_each_beat
   Beyond convenience, discontinuity allows the best camera angle to be chosen for each emotion and each story point, and those choices to be assembled for a cumulatively greater impact. A continuous stream of images would make this difficult and films would not be as sharp or as to the point.

== sk_murch_c003_0010 | murch_blink_p1_25_editing_decisions | cutting_is_a_positive_force_not_merely_a_repair
   Cutting is not only the means by which discontinuous shooting is made to read as continuous. By the very force of its paradoxical suddenness it is in and for itself a positive influence on a film, and the source states we would want to cut even if discontinuity had no practical value at all.

== sk_murch_c003_0012 | murch_blink_p1_25_editing_decisions | removal_is_constrained_by_the_structure_of_what_remains
   A bad bit may only be removed if removing it does not disrupt the structure of the good material that remains. Identifying what to cut is therefore not sufficient warrant for cutting it.

== sk_murch_c003_0014 | murch_blink_p1_25_editing_decisions | do_not_change_what_kind_of_film_you_are_making_midway
   Once a film's kind is set, changing it during production produces something that is neither. The source names two failure patterns from its own viewing: an unpretentious film whose possibilities someone inflated mid-production, which became boring and pretentious; and an ambitious film to which a studio ordered action and sex added, reducing a great potential to something less. Both are described as neither human nor chimp.

== sk_murch_c003_0017 | murch_blink_p1_25_editing_decisions | deciding_not_to_cut_is_the_larger_part_of_the_work
   A film is not better for having more cuts in it, and it frequently takes more work and discernment to decide where not to cut than where to cut. The source reframes the editor as making twenty-four decisions a second, almost all of them negative — the cut is the one moment the answer is yes. An editor is paid to make decisions, not to cut.

== sk_murch_c003_0018 | murch_blink_p1_25_editing_decisions | complete_control_of_the_viewers_attention_is_self_defeating
   An editor who changes shots too frequently is like a tour guide who cannot stop pointing things out. Some of the time the viewer wants to be shown; some of the time they want to walk around and see what they see. An editor without the confidence to let people occasionally choose what to look at, or to leave things to their imagination, is pursuing complete control, which is self-defeating: viewers eventually feel constrained and then resentful under the constant pressure of his hand on the backs of their necks.

== sk_murch_c003_0019 | murch_blink_p1_25_editing_decisions | an_ideal_cut_satisfies_six_ranked_criteria_at_once
   An ideal cut satisfies six criteria simultaneously: it is true to the emotion of the moment, it advances the story, it occurs at a rhythmically interesting and right moment, it acknowledges eye-trace, it respects planarity, and it respects the three-dimensional continuity of the actual space. The six are not equal — the source presents them as a ranked list of priorities governing what to give up first when they cannot all be satisfied.

== sk_murch_c003_0020 | murch_blink_p1_25_editing_decisions | emotion_ranks_first_and_is_worth_more_than_the_other_five_combined
   Emotion is the first criterion, weighted by the source at 51 percent — more than the other five combined. A cut should be true to the emotion of the moment, and emotion should be preserved at all costs. The source states it is the thing film school comes to last, if at all, because it is the hardest to define and deal with, and that what an audience finally remembers is not the editing, the camerawork, the performances or even the story, but how they felt.

== sk_murch_c003_0021 | murch_blink_p1_25_editing_decisions | story_ranks_second_at_twenty_three_percent
   Advancing the story is the second criterion, weighted at 23 percent. With emotion it forms the top two, which the source says are worth far more than the bottom four together.

== sk_murch_c003_0022 | murch_blink_p1_25_editing_decisions | rhythm_ranks_third_at_ten_percent
   Rhythm is the third criterion, weighted at 10 percent: the cut should occur at a moment that is rhythmically interesting and right.

== sk_murch_c003_0023 | murch_blink_p1_25_editing_decisions | eye_trace_ranks_fourth_at_seven_percent
   Eye-trace is the fourth criterion, weighted at 7 percent, and the source defines it: the concern with the location and movement of the audience's focus of interest within the frame.

== sk_murch_c003_0030 | murch_blink_p1_25_editing_decisions | the_top_three_criteria_are_tightly_bound_and_the_lower_bonds_weaken
   Emotion, story and rhythm are extremely tightly connected in practice — the source likens the forces binding them to the bonds between protons and neutrons in an atomic nucleus, by far the tightest — and the forces connecting the lower three grow progressively weaker down the list.

== sk_murch_c003_0031 | murch_blink_p1_25_editing_decisions | all_six_are_usually_satisfiable_and_should_always_be_aimed_at
   Most of the time all six criteria can be satisfied at once — three-dimensional space, the two-dimensional plane, eye-trace, rhythm, story and emotion all falling into place — and the editor should always aim for that, never accepting less when more is available.

== sk_murch_c003_0033 | murch_blink_p1_25_editing_decisions | the_editor_misdirects_attention_as_a_magician_does
   Keeping the audience's mental state in view is also the preoccupation of every magician, and the editor is a kind of everyday working magician. The source's case is Houdini, whose job was to create wonder and who therefore found ways to make the audience look left while he undid his chains on the right — misdirection, in the magicians' term. An editor can, does and should do the same.

== sk_murch_c003_0035 | murch_blink_p1_25_editing_decisions | presence_at_the_shoot_makes_you_see_past_the_frame
   Anyone who was on the set — actors, producer, director, cameraman, art director — cannot help seeing, in the mind's eye, around the edge of the frame: imagining everything that was there physically and emotionally, just beyond what was actually photographed. The editor is one of the few people with substantial influence on a film who does not know, or has the ability not to know, the exact conditions of shooting. The source treats this ignorance as the editor's qualification, not a deficiency.

== sk_murch_c003_0036 | murch_blink_p1_25_editing_decisions | the_cost_of_a_shot_distorts_judgement_in_both_directions
   What a shot cost distorts how it is judged, and in both directions. A shot that took great money, time and angst to get may be defended as what was wanted, when the conviction comes from the cost rather than from the result. Conversely, a shot taken under protest, in a bad mood, at the end of a difficult day, may be dismissed on the memory of the hateful moment it was shot, leaving its potential in a different context unseen.

== sk_gote_c003_0001 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | edit_for_the_specific_targeted_audience
   Every editing decision is made on behalf of a particular intended audience, not an audience in general. The material was written, shot and must be cut with that audience in mind. Anticipating what they will want to see next is presented as the editor's primary orienting skill, and different genres and audiences require different editing approaches.

== sk_gote_c003_0002 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | audiences_read_edits_through_learned_convention
   Viewers acquire, from long exposure to film and television, a subconscious competence at reading edits. They decipher meaning in the flow of images without being aware of doing so. This learned competence is what the book calls a grammar: just as shot types carry meaning, the way shots are joined carries meaning to the viewer.

== sk_gote_c003_0003 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | six_factors_that_make_a_transition_a_good_edit
   Six factors are named as the major reasons to consider a cut and as the checklist against which a transition is judged: information, motivation, shot composition, camera angle, continuity and sound. The source presents them as a starting point rather than an exhaustive account.

== sk_gote_c003_0004 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | every_new_shot_must_carry_new_information
   Each shot cut to must give the viewer information it did not already have. The information may be visual — a character entering, a new location, an unexplained event — or aural, such as narration or an off-screen sound. The source states this is basic to all editing choices, and frames the editor's working question as: what would, should, cannot and do I wish the audience to see next.

== sk_gote_c003_0005 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | discard_a_shot_that_adds_no_information_however_good
   A shot's quality, appeal or cost does not earn it a place in the cut. If it adds no new information to the story's progression it may not belong in the final edit, and the editor should look for another shot from the same scene that does.

== sk_gote_c003_0006 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | every_departure_from_a_shot_must_be_motivated
   Information governs the shot being cut to; motivation governs the shot being cut away from. There must always be a reason to leave the current shot and a reason it is left at that particular moment. The motivating element may be visual — usually some movement by a subject or object, from a car clearing a river down to a small shift of a character's eyes — or aural.

== sk_gote_c003_0007 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | sound_bridge
   A sound whose source is not visible or known begins under the outgoing shot, motivates the cut, and is explained by the incoming shot, which reveals its origin. The device carries the viewer across a change of location or subject that would otherwise be abrupt.

== sk_gote_c003_0008 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | apparent_object_size_sets_its_sound_level
   How large an object appears in frame governs how loud its sound should be in the mix. Cutting closer magnifies the object's visual size and importance, so raising the level of the sound it makes is appropriate.

== sk_gote_c003_0009 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | elapsed_time_as_motivator_measured_in_beats
   With experience, the passage of time itself becomes a sufficient motivation to cut. The interval is not counted in seconds or frames but felt as a unit the source calls a beat — described as an unknowable yet knowable amount, a feeling that it is now time to cut to something different.

== sk_gote_c003_0010 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | alternating_frame_placement_drives_eye_trace_across_the_cut
   The editor cannot compose the shots but chooses which compositions meet at a cut. Placing one character at frame left with open look room to the right, and cutting to a matching shot of the other character at frame right with look room to the left, makes the viewer's gaze travel across the screen at the cut and land on the new face. The source calls this eye-line match or eye trace and likens the effect to a tennis ball crossing a court.

== sk_gote_c003_0011 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | eye_trace_difficulty_has_an_optimum
   The visual search the viewer performs at a cut has a useful range. A subtle search sustains interest; an elaborate one produces confusion. Beautiful multi-layered compositions can therefore work against the edit, and the editor must consider how the audience will locate the important new information inside a busy frame.

== sk_gote_c003_0012 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | successive_shots_need_more_than_thirty_degrees_of_angle_difference
   Two shots joined at a cut must be taken from horizontal camera angles more than about 30 degrees apart around the 180 degree arc. The editor cannot control where the camera was placed and can only choose, from the coverage supplied, shots whose angles differ enough.

== sk_gote_c003_0013 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | jump_cut_as_a_consciously_noticed_break
   A jump cut is what the viewer perceives when successive images are too alike: a jump, glitch or very brief interruption. Its defining property is that the viewer notices it consciously, which pulls them out of the viewing experience — something the editor should almost always prevent.

== sk_gote_c003_0014 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | continuity_is_four_separable_kinds
   Continuity across a transition is not one property but four that must be addressed separately: continuity of content, of movement, of position and of sound. The editor is not responsible for the quality of the footage but is responsible for making the best picture possible from it, and must compensate in the edit where production failed to supply matching material.

== sk_gote_c003_0015 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | continuity_of_content_actions_and_objects_must_match
   Actions and the objects involved in them must match from shot to shot within a scene: a dog present in the wide shot should be present in the tighter shots, a telephone picked up in the right hand should still be in the right hand after the cut. Where the coverage does not supply matching material the editor must hide, mask or explain the incongruity, and the audience must remain unaware of it.

== sk_gote_c003_0016 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | cut_away_buys_time_for_the_audience_to_accept_a_discontinuity
   Inserting any brief shot between two mismatched shots gives the audience both a distraction and enough elapsed time to accept that the change happened off screen. The same device is named twice in the chapter: as a cut-away for an object or action mismatch, and as an insert shot for a reversal of screen direction.

== sk_gote_c003_0017 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | continuity_of_movement_screen_direction_maintained
   Screen direction — whether talent or objects move toward frame left or frame right — must hold across a cut whenever the next shot continues the same movement. Where the available shot contradicts the established direction, the editor must insert a shot that continues the narrative and breaks the audience's attention on the direction of travel.

== sk_gote_c003_0018 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | continuity_of_position_subject_keeps_its_side_of_the_frame
   Film space has a sense of place as well as direction. A subject or object occupying one side of the frame must remain on that side in subsequent shots of the scene, unless it physically moved during a shot, in which case showing it on a new side is logical. Cutting two shots that make a subject jump across the screen distracts the viewer and breaks the illusion of smooth editing.

== sk_gote_c003_0019 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | continuity_of_sound_and_perspective_across_the_cut
   When shots share a time and place, their sound continues across the cut whether or not its source remains visible: an aeroplane heard in the first shot is still audible to the characters in the second and should still be audible to the audience. Sound levels for voices and objects should stay consistent through an edited scene, and changes in an object's distance from camera should be represented by raising or lowering its level.

== sk_gote_c003_0020 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | ambience_bed_smooths_audio_transitions
   Every space has an ever-present background sound level — ambience, also called atmosphere, natural sound or nats, and when lifted from production recordings, room tone. It creates a bed of consistent tone under dialogue and effects, and laying it continuously across cuts smooths the audio transitions within a scene. It is either taken from the production recordings or built by an editor or sound designer from other sources.

== sk_gote_c003_0022 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | split_edit_offsets_picture_and_sound_cut_points
   Picture and sound are separate tracks and need not change at the same instant. Cutting both at once is a butt-cut or straight cut. Offsetting one produces a split edit, also called an L-cut or lapping: sound leading picture when the next shot's audio arrives first, picture leading sound when the outgoing audio continues under the new image. Assemble and rough cuts are normally all butt-cuts; split edits appear when timing is refined in the fine cut.

== sk_gote_c003_0024 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | each_transition_type_carries_its_own_learned_meaning
   The cut, the dissolve, the wipe and the fade are not interchangeable ways of getting from one shot to the next. Each carries its own meaning when seen within a programme, and audiences hold expectations about how each is properly, or at least more commonly, used. The source announces that it will analyse all four against the six elements introduced in the previous chapter.

== sk_gote_c003_0025 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | the_cut_is_instantaneous_and_transparent_when_well_made
   The cut is an instantaneous change from one shot to another and the most frequently used transition. Made at the right moment and satisfying as many of the positive edit elements as possible, it is not consciously noticed — it is transparent — and it is the one transition audiences have come to accept as a form of visual reality. The source also gives its etymology: film strips were physically cut, so editors were called cutters, and the word survives.

== sk_gote_c003_0026 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | composition_difference_is_required_at_the_cut_independently_of_subject
   Two shots joined at a cut must differ in composition, and this holds even when their subject matter is entirely different. Similar compositions read as a visual jump cut regardless of content. A difference in composition makes the viewer engage and search the new frame for the new information, and while they are searching they do not notice the cut.

== sk_gote_c003_0027 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | when_to_use_a_cut
   A cut is the appropriate transition in three situations: where the action is continuous, where a change of impact is needed, and where there is a change of information or locale. The source also positions the cut as the default working transition — the assemble edit should always be built from cuts, and it remains the fastest way to work.

== sk_gote_c003_0028 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | dissolve_duration_governs_how_it_reads
   How long a dissolve lingers determines what the audience reads it as. A dissolve of only a few overlapping frames passes so quickly it can imitate a jump cut if the other edit elements are not met. A dissolve lasting several seconds can, around its midpoint, read as a superimposition rather than a transition. One second is the usual software default; a dissolve may last as long as there is material on both shots, and should last as long as its purpose requires.

== sk_gote_c003_0029 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | the_dissolve_is_a_deliberately_visible_manipulation_of_time
   A dissolve is a gradual change from the end of one shot into the beginning of the next, achieved by superimposing both while ramping one down in opacity and the other up, so the images are seen overlapping. Unlike the cut it attracts attention to itself on purpose. Its chief use is to play with time — condensing a long event into a montage, moving into a flashback, or slowing events with slow motion so the audience has time to absorb them. Also called a lap dissolve, a lap, or a video mix.

== sk_gote_c003_0030 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | when_to_use_a_dissolve
   Five conditions call for a dissolve: a change in time, a change in locale, a need to slow or speed time, an emotional appeal concerning the subject, or a strong visual relationship between the outgoing and incoming images. The source's composition guidance adds that the two shots should overlap without visual contradiction at the midpoint, either by opposing frames that balance or by matching frames of different subjects — the match dissolve.

== sk_gote_c003_0031 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | dissolves_carry_a_languid_somber_thoughtful_register
   Dissolves carry an emotional register of their own. They are commonly called the tear jerker transition and are associated with languid, somber or thoughtful responses, and they are effective in romantic or sad sequences, particularly with slow motion.

== sk_gote_c003_0032 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | transition_conventions_are_genre_bound_and_historically_unstable
   Which transitions are appropriate depends on genre, and the boundaries move. Dissolves belong in fiction, television drama and music video but are rare in daily news, because a device understood to represent the manipulation of time has little legitimate place in factual reporting. The source states that even this rule of visual grammar is changing.

Wipes are meant to be noticed, often appear in shapes or with graphic elements, and can zigzag, spiral or travel diagonally, horizontally or vertically. == sk_gote_c003_0036 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | natural_wipe_uses_in_frame_movement_as_the_wiping_element
   Where the outgoing shot contains strong vertical or horizontal movement, an object within the action can appear to push, pull or wipe across the screen, and the editor can use that movement as the transition itself. == sk_gote_c003_0037 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | the_fade_marks_the_boundary_of_a_program_or_act
   A fade in reveals the image from an opaque field, usually black; a fade out returns it to one. Their conventional function is to signal that a story, chapter, scene, sequence or act has begun or ended, and they are also used where there is a change in time or locale. Scripts conventionally open with "fade in" and close with "fade out". The source describes them as transitions into and out of the dream-like state of motion picture viewing.

== sk_gote_c003_0038 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | low_contrast_frames_fade_evenly_high_contrast_frames_do_not
   A fade reads as clean when the shot at the fade point is low in contrast. Large areas of dark and light within the frame make the fade appear uneven or poorly timed, because as opacity gradually fills in or removes the image the difference in brightness between those areas creates an imbalance.

== sk_gote_c003_0039 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | five_categories_of_edit_type_named_without_claim_to_standard
   Edits fall into five named categories — action, screen position, form, concept and combined — which cover most of the major kinds of edit performable with most material. The source states the list is not exhaustive, that there may be no industry standard for the terminology, and that the definitions nevertheless apply generally. All five are said to benefit from the same six elements that strengthen any edit.

== sk_gote_c003_0040 | grammar_of_the_edit_ch3_5_editing_decisions | action_edit_joins_continuous_movement_across_the_cut
   An action edit joins two shots depicting one continuous action or movement, with different framings and unbroken time, so the movement appears smooth and continuous across the cut. It is nearly always a straight cut, and is also called a movement edit or a continuity edit. The continuity of movement must match at the cut point, and the editor is free to cut at any point during the overlapping action.

   A screen position edit uses the placement of subjects in two shots to move the viewer's eyes around the screen. One strong element occupies one side of the frame and directs attention or movement toward the other; the incoming shot puts the object of that attention on the opposite side, satisfying the viewer's need to see something occupy that space. Also called a directional or placement edit. It can be a cut or a dissolve, but is usually a cut where no passage of time is implied.

== sk_wcag_0014 | w3c-wcag22-text-legibility | contrast_ratio_is_a_relative_luminance_formula_bounded_at_1_and_21
   Contrast ratio is defined as (L1 + 0.05) / (L2 + 0.05), where L1 is the relative luminance of the lighter colour and L2 that of the darker. Because the lighter colour is always the numerator, the measure is symmetric — dark text on a light ground and its inverse give the same number. The source's first note bounds the scale: ratios range from 1 to 21, commonly written 1:1 to 21:1. The quantity is dimensionless and is a property of a pair of colours, not of a single colour.
