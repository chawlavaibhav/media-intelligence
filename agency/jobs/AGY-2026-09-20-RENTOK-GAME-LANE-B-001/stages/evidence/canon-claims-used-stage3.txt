
== sk_wcag_0004 | logotype_text_has_no_contrast_requirement
   Text forming part of a logo or brand name is exempt from the contrast requirements of SC 1.4.3 and SC 1.4.6 entirely. The criterion states the exemption without qualification.

== sk_wcag_0007 | colour_must_not_be_the_only_visual_means
   Colour must not be the only visual means of conveying information, indicating an action, prompting a response, or distinguishing a visual element. The criterion is about colour perception specifically; the source's own note directs other forms of perception, including programmatic access to colour, to Guideline 1.3. This is a Level A criterion — the lowest conformance level, and therefore the most broadly binding of the criteria in this extraction.

== sk_wcag_0022 | thin_or_unfamiliar_letterforms_are_harder_to_read_especially_at_lower_contrast
   Fonts with extraordinarily thin strokes, or unusual features and characteristics that reduce the familiarity of their letterforms, are harder to read — and the source specifies that this is especially so at lower contrast levels. Stroke weight and letterform familiarity interact with contrast rather than being independent of it.

== sk_ogx_0007 | name_the_audience_or_the_place_in_the_headline_to_flag_the_right_reader
   Where a product is bought only by a small group, the headline should contain a word that flags those people down. The same self-selection logic is applied to place: local newspaper advertisements do better when the headline names the city, because people are mostly interested in what happens where they live.

== sk_ogx_0009 | specifics_are_more_credible_than_generalities_and_superlatives_convince_nobody
   Specifics beat generalities because they are more credible and more memorable. The source's worked case is a Sears headline: research had found the average shopper believed Sears made a thirty-seven per cent profit on sales, so he headlined an advertisement with the actual figure of five per cent rather than saying the profit was less than one might suppose. At the other end of the same axis he places what Gallup called Brag and Boast — superlatives such as best in the world — which he says convince nobody.

== sk_whip_0029 | puns_and_rhymes_are_named_as_having_no_persuasive_value
   Sullivan states flatly that puns have no persuasive value and calls them the lowest thing on the joke food chain, and that a headline that rhymes is beyond saving. His instruction is not to avoid thinking of them but to get them out of the system early: it is fine to write them down, provided they are then thrown away. He treats the pun as belonging to the same family as the visual cliché and the low-hanging fruit — a form that arrives first and must be cleared before the real search begins.

== sk_whip_0033 | emphatic_punctuation_reads_as_desperation_rather_than_enthusiasm
   Sullivan makes a claim about how a mark is read rather than about taste: the writer intends an exclamation point to sound excited, and the reader receives it as a brand sounding desperate. He states the prohibition absolutely and demonstrates it by making the point emphatically without one. The stated cost is to authenticity — he calls the mark kryptonite to it, which links this to his wider argument that inauthentic signals are detected and discounted.

== sk_nnn_0052 | hindi_originated_copy_moved_from_translation_department_to_the_centre
   The author records a structural change in who writes Indian advertising. Through the 1960s and 1970s the business was dominated by English-medium, convent-educated writers who had succeeded the departing British admen, and Indian-language writers were confined to a separate "Language Department" that translated. From the mid-1970s writers such as Kamlesh Pandey began creating work ORIGINALLY in Hindi rather than translating into it, and the author names Piyush Pandey, Balki and Prasoon Joshi as continuing that through the 1990s and 2000s.

== sk_ppm_0001 | the_authors_stated_communication_principle
   Pandey states his governing communication principle in the Preface, drawn from his father's habit of improvising verse to the household each morning: say it with respect for the audience, in a context the audience can understand, spontaneously, without fear, and to delight rather than to intimidate. He calls these his "kindergarten lessons in communication" and the book returns to them.
