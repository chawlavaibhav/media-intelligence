# Canon context

## the three reflection types as a contrast set
_Source: light-science-magic-ch3 · scs_lsm_c003_001_

Diffuse, direct and polarized direct reflection are defined against one another on the same four questions: does viewing angle matter, does source distance matter, does source size matter, and how bright is the result. Every real surface produces a mixture, and the mixture's proportions are what make one surface look unlike another.

The three reflection types form one contrast set, not three separate topics. Each is defined by how it answers the same questions, they behave oppositely on several of them, and no real surface exhibits one alone. What a photographer sees is always a proportion.

## family of angles as a placement control
_Source: light-science-magic-ch3 · scs_lsm_c003_002_

A physical derivation ending in a placement rule. Rays reflect at the angle they strike; that fixes a single viewing angle per point; summed across a surface it becomes the family of angles; whether a light falls inside or outside that family decides whether a direct reflection appears at all; and a source large enough to fill the family produces reflection across the whole surface.

Direct reflection is not a property of a surface but of a geometric relationship between source, surface and camera. Because that relationship can be calculated, the photographer has a two-sided control: put the light inside the family to produce the reflection, or place camera and light so it falls outside to remove it.

## terminological discipline as a condition of the theory
_Source: light-science-magic-ch3 · scs_lsm_c003_003_

The chapter twice stops to intervene in its own field's vocabulary: it refuses the word specular because practitioners use it for at least three different things, and it separates two senses of diffusion because one describes the light and the other the surface. Both interventions exist to protect the same distinction — that the source determines the type of light and the surface determines the type of reflection.

This chapter's physical account only works if one distinction holds: the light and the surface are separate causes with separate vocabularies. Both terminological interventions defend that distinction, and the source treats getting the words right as a precondition of getting the physics right rather than as a stylistic preference.

## what illumination is for
_Source: alton-painting-with-light-ch2 · scs_alt_c003_003_

The source's own statement of purpose — quantity, meaning correct exposure everywhere, and quality, meaning four numbered values: orientation, mood, pictorial beauty and depth — together with what it goes on to say about each: the camera as the audience's eye under orientation, the genre key and the dramatic contour under mood, and the dark-to-light progression under depth.

Quantity and quality are stated as separable, and the source sharpens the separation later by insisting that a daylight feeling cannot be produced by overexposing: mood comes from the character of the light, not its level. The four quality values are numbered but never ranked, and the chapter's own material shows them competing — the genre key can override the requirement that light agree with its source, and the two depth rules pull in different directions.
