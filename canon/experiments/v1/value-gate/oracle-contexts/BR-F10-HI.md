# Canon context

## how tightly the frame holds the subject
_Source: freeman-photographers-eye-graphic-guide · scs_fre_c003_002_

Four treatments of the same variable — how exactly the frame is made to meet the subject. Exact framing cuts to measured proportions; frame-fit makes the subject just fit the frame; frame break deliberately halves it at the edge; loose framing stops attending to the edges at all.

Attention to the frame's edges is a dial, not a standard. Its correct setting depends on what is happening inside: where the picture's interest is dense and active, the edges cease to be noticed and precision buys nothing; where the subject is isolated and controlled, the edges carry the picture. The source never states this as a principle, but it pairs the cases explicitly — "this is the opposite of the previous image" — and its justification for loose framing is exactly that the edges stop mattering.

## the rule of six
_Source: murch-blink-p1-25 · scs_murch_c003_001_

Six criteria for an ideal cut, numbered by the source and ranked: emotion, story, rhythm, eye-trace, planarity, three-dimensional continuity. The source attaches a percentage to each (51, 23, 10, 7, 5, 4), states a procedure for when they conflict — sacrifice upward from the bottom — and gives two claims about how the ranks relate to each other: that higher criteria obscure failures of lower ones but not the reverse, and that the top three are bound far more tightly to each other than the lower three are.

The six are a single ordered instrument, not six separate pieces of advice. The order is what makes it usable: it tells an editor what to give up first when a cut cannot satisfy everything, and the source states it in that form — "What I'm suggesting is a list of priorities." The ordering is also claimed to be perceptual and not merely preferential: satisfying a higher item tends to obscure problems with lower items, and not the other way round. And the source ranks three-dimensional spatial continuity LAST, deliberately reversing what it says film schools teach first and what early sound film treated as the rule.

## the three aspects as a sequential test
_Source: vignelli-canon-intangibles · scs_vig_c003_001_

Semantics, syntactics and pragmatics are presented as three aspects to be examined one at a time, and the source treats them as successive conditions: find the meaning, make the elements consistent with one another, and check that the result is understood. Failing the third voids the first two.

Work can be semantically correct and syntactically consistent and still be wasted effort, if at the point of fruition no one understands it. The three aspects are therefore cumulative conditions rather than parallel concerns, and comprehension is the one that can void the others.

## the four continuities
_Source: grammar-of-the-edit-ch3-5 · scs_gote_c003_003_

Continuity is divided by the source into four kinds that must each be addressed separately: content, movement, position and sound. Each has its own failure and, for three of the four, its own remedy.

The four are treated as one responsibility with four independent failure surfaces. A transition can satisfy any three and fail the fourth, and each failure looks different to the viewer — a mismatched object, a reversed direction of travel, a subject that jumps across the screen, sound that vanishes. Two of the four, movement and position, share a single underlying cause in the geometry of the action line, and the source treats them separately in Chapter Three before supplying the shared cause in Chapter Five.
