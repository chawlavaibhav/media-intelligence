# Canon context

## the rule of six
_Source: murch-blink-p1-25 · scs_murch_c003_001_

Six criteria for an ideal cut, numbered by the source and ranked: emotion, story, rhythm, eye-trace, planarity, three-dimensional continuity. The source attaches a percentage to each (51, 23, 10, 7, 5, 4), states a procedure for when they conflict — sacrifice upward from the bottom — and gives two claims about how the ranks relate to each other: that higher criteria obscure failures of lower ones but not the reverse, and that the top three are bound far more tightly to each other than the lower three are.

The six are a single ordered instrument, not six separate pieces of advice. The order is what makes it usable: it tells an editor what to give up first when a cut cannot satisfy everything, and the source states it in that form — "What I'm suggesting is a list of priorities." The ordering is also claimed to be perceptual and not merely preferential: satisfying a higher item tends to obscure problems with lower items, and not the other way round. And the source ranks three-dimensional spatial continuity LAST, deliberately reversing what it says film schools teach first and what early sound film treated as the rule.

## the seven general practices
_Source: grammar-of-the-edit-ch3-5 · scs_gote_c003_005_

Seven practices the source lists as the starting point for approaching any material: sound and vision are partners not rivals; a new shot should contain new information; there should be a reason for every edit; observe the action line; select the appropriate form of edit; the better the edit, the less it is noticed; editing is creating.

Six of the seven restate material already taught as elements or transitions, and the source says so — it flags two of them as being elements of the cut and the dissolve. What the list adds is a change of register: the same content stated as standing practice rather than as a per-transition test, plus one practice with no counterpart earlier, the invisibility standard, and one that is not a practice at all but a claim about the editor's role. The list is the source's own; treating it as mostly a restatement with two additions is ours.

## the attention economy of the soundtrack
_Source: ondaatje-conversations-ch3 · scs_conv_c003_001_

A set of claims about sound that all turn on where a finite audience attention is directed and what the audience believes about where a sound came from: that music works best channelling an emotion the film has already created; that music with a visible in-scene origin evades the listener's sense of being manipulated while a score does not; that withdrawing dialogue makes the audience search the remaining sound for meaning; and that acoustic perspective can therefore carry plot.

Sound in this source is governed by two variables, and every claim in the group is a consequence of one of them. The first is ATTENTION, which is finite and competitive: "You pay attention to the stars on nights when there is no moon… Dialogue is the moon, and stars are the sound effects." Remove dialogue and unchanged effects acquire significance. The second is ATTRIBUTION: a sound the audience can assign to the world of the scene is not experienced as authored, so it passes without resistance, while a sound with no in-world origin registers as a new voice and the viewer repositions. Together they explain why the strongest sound effects in this chapter are subtractive — dropping dialogue, dropping a score, an echo that does not match its space — rather than additive.
