# Craft context

## When two requirements cannot both be met in one shot
Some briefs contain requirements that are physically incompatible within a single frame. Showing
fine detail on a small object and showing a wide, expansive scene are the clearest example: the
closer the framing, the more detail is legible and the less context is visible. This is not a
matter of preference and cannot be solved by compromise, because a middle distance delivers
neither.

## Resolving across shots rather than within one
The normal resolution is to give each requirement its own shot. A wide frame establishes scale
and setting; a close frame delivers the detail. Sequenced together, the viewer receives both. A
plan should state this explicitly rather than presenting a single compromise framing and hoping
it satisfies the brief.

## How tightly to frame
How much space surrounds a subject changes what the image is about. A tight frame makes the
subject the entire subject and removes context; a loose frame places it in a world and reduces
its prominence. Choosing this deliberately, shot by shot, is one of the more consequential
decisions available.

## Deciding between competing registers
A request for two opposed styles — grand and contemporary, ornate and restrained — needs a
decision rather than an average. Usually one register should structure the piece while the other
appears in specific, limited ways. Say which is leading and why.

## Consistency across a short sequence
Across several shots the same person must remain recognisably the same, wearing the same things,
in the same setting, under consistent light. Where jewellery, clothing or ornament is the subject,
it must be identical from shot to shot; changes read immediately as errors.

## Cultural specificity
Where a piece depends on a particular cultural context — an occasion, its conventions, what is
appropriate within it — accuracy matters and cannot be inferred from general design principles.
Where that knowledge is not available, the honest course is to identify what needs to be verified
rather than to approximate it.

## Planning a short sequence of shots
With only three or four shots available, each one has to earn its place. A workable approach assigns
each shot a distinct job — establish the setting, show the subject, deliver the detail, close on the
brand — so that none duplicates another. Listing those jobs before choosing framings prevents a
sequence of attractive but redundant images.

## Showing ornament and fine detail
Small, reflective or intricate objects are described by how light moves across them. They generally
need a light source that is directional enough to reveal form and broad enough not to produce harsh
glare, and a framing close enough that the detail occupies real space in the frame rather than a few
pixels of it.

## Wardrobe and continuity
Where costume and ornament are the subject, every element must match exactly across shots. This is
one of the most common continuity failures in sequences involving dress, because each shot looks
correct in isolation.

## Text and brand at the close
A business name and location shown at the end must be legible at viewing size and accurate. Where
this text is supplied it should be reproduced exactly, and its legibility checked against whatever
sits behind it.

## Before finishing
Confirm the required detail is legible somewhere, that the sequence holds together, and that
every stated requirement has been addressed or explicitly raised.
