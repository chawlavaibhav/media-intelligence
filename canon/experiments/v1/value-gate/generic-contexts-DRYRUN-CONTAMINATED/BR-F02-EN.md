# Craft context

## Photographing a product so it reads correctly
A product shot has to do two things at once: present the object attractively and describe it
accurately. When those pull apart, accuracy generally has to win, because a customer who receives
something different from the image is a customer lost. Decide early which properties of the
object the image must convey — its material, its size, its finish, what it is made of — and treat
those as requirements rather than as aesthetic preferences.

## Materials and how light behaves on them
Different surfaces need different treatment. A glossy or transparent object shows the light source
itself in its surface, so the shape, size and position of that source becomes part of the object's
appearance. Broad, soft light tends to produce long gradual highlights that describe a curved
surface; small hard sources produce bright compact spots that can read as glare and can obscure
what lies beneath. Transparent objects are usually described by what shows through them and by the
edges where their material bends light, rather than by light falling on their front.

## Keeping labels and text legible
Where a label carries the product's claim, its legibility is a functional requirement of the
image. Reflections that fall across text are the usual cause of failure. So is a viewing angle
that foreshortens the label, and a background whose tone sits too close to the label's own.

## Background and context
A clean background keeps attention on the object and is generally the right choice when the
product is unfamiliar. It also removes any cue about scale, so if size matters it has to be
conveyed another way. Whatever the background, it should not introduce colours that contaminate
the product's own.

## Composition
Give the object enough space that it does not feel cramped, but not so much that it looks small
and unimportant. A slightly elevated three-quarter view describes most objects better than a
straight-on view, because it shows more than one surface and therefore more of the form.

## Colour accuracy
Where a product's colour is part of its identity, the image has to represent it faithfully. Colour
shifts introduced by the light source, by a coloured surface bouncing light back onto the object, or
by heavy processing will all misrepresent it. Neutral lighting and a neutral environment make the
product's own colour readable, and where a brand colour is specified it should be treated as a
requirement to be checked rather than an approximate target.

## Consistency across a range
A product shot rarely lives alone. It will sit beside other products from the same business, in a
feed, on a listing page or in a catalogue. Establishing a repeatable approach — the same angle, the
same lighting arrangement, the same distance and background — matters more than optimising any single
image. An inconsistent set reads as amateur even when every individual frame is competent.

## Before finishing
Confirm the object looks like what it actually is, that any required text is readable, and that
nothing in the frame contradicts a claim the copy makes.
