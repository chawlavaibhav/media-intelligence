# Craft context

## People in commercial imagery
When a person appears with a product, the viewer reads the relationship between them before
reading anything else. Decide what that relationship should say — that the person uses the
product, trusts it, has been helped by it — and let the pose, expression and framing serve that.
A person who looks like they are presenting a product to camera reads as an advertisement; a
person who looks like they are using it reads as evidence.

## Credibility in sensitive categories
Some categories carry consequences for the customer, and money is the clearest of them. In those,
a viewer's first question is whether the business can be trusted, and everything in the creative
either supports or undermines that. Visual signals of seriousness — restraint, clarity,
consistency, accurate detail — do more for trust than statements of trustworthiness do.

## Reconciling opposed tones
Briefs frequently ask for two tones that work against each other, such as authoritative and
playful. Averaging them tends to produce something bland. A better approach is to decide which
tone carries the responsibility of the message and which is allowed to colour the surface — for
instance, a serious structure with a warm, informal voice. State that decision so it can be
reviewed rather than leaving it implicit.

## Interfaces shown inside creatives
When a screen appears in an advertisement it becomes a factual claim about the product. It should
be the real interface, current, and legible at the size it will be viewed. An invented or
outdated screen is both misleading and, in practice, the detail that customers notice.

## Assuming knowledge the audience lacks
People who work on a product forget what it is like not to understand it. Terms that feel basic
inside a company are often opaque outside it. Test the main message against someone with no prior
knowledge, and prefer plain description of the benefit over category vocabulary.

## Calls to action
If the purpose of the creative is to prompt an action, that action must be visible, specific and
easy. A viewer who is persuaded but unsure what to do next is a wasted impression.

## Framing for the platform
A creative made for a feed will be seen small, quickly, and often with other content directly above
and below it. Decide early what the piece must communicate at that size, and check the composition
by viewing it small rather than at full resolution. Faces and text survive scale reduction better
than fine detail and thin type.

## Casting and representation
Who appears in a creative tells the audience who it is for. The person shown should be plausibly a
member of the audience being addressed rather than an idealised figure, particularly where the
proposition is about accessibility or ease. Wardrobe, setting and grooming all contribute to this
and should be chosen deliberately rather than defaulting to the most polished option available.

## Legibility of overlaid text
Text placed over an image competes with whatever is behind it. Reserving a clear area for the copy
at the composition stage works better than adding a darkening layer afterwards, which tends to make
the image muddy while only partly solving the problem.

## Before finishing
Check that the person, the product and the message tell the same story, and that nothing shown
would be contradicted by the actual product experience.
