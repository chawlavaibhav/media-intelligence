# Craft context

## When the brief does not fit the duration
A fixed duration is a hard constraint, and requirements have to fit inside it. When a brief asks
for more than the time allows — several lines, a product reveal, character establishment and a
closing card in a very short piece — the honest response is to say so and offer options: remove a
requirement, extend the duration, or simplify one element. Silently dropping something is the
common failure, and it surfaces only at review.

## Deciding what to give up first
Not all requirements cost the same. Some carry the message; others support it. Rank them
explicitly, decide what can be lost with least damage, and state that reasoning. A plan that
shows its priorities can be corrected; one that presents a reduced scope as if it were the full
brief cannot.

## Establishing people quickly
Character comes across through behaviour rather than through description. One clear action or one
well-chosen line does more than several seconds of screen time. In a short piece there is no room
for introduction, so the first thing each person does has to carry who they are.

## Objection and answer
A structure where one person voices a doubt the audience shares, and another answers it, is
efficient because it does the audience's thinking out loud. The doubt has to be the real one, and
the answer has to be specific enough to be believed.

## Tone in categories involving money
Humour gains attention; it can also undercut confidence when a significant purchase is involved.
A workable approach lets the lighter tone carry the setup and the more serious tone carry the
answer, so the piece is approachable without seeming casual about the customer's risk.

## Rules are guides, not laws
Conventional practice exists because it usually works, not because it always does. Where a stated
purpose is better served by departing from convention, that is legitimate — provided the
departure is deliberate and stated rather than accidental.

## Location and context
Where the exchange happens should support what is being discussed without demanding attention. A
setting directly relevant to the subject does useful work; a busy or visually complex one costs the
viewer attention they need for the dialogue.

## Delivering exact lines naturally
Where dialogue is supplied verbatim it must be delivered as written, but the performance around it
can still feel unforced. Reaction, timing and the moment before a line often do more than the line
itself, and they cost no extra duration if planned into the shot rather than added afterwards.

## Showing a product or service credibly
Where an application or service is the subject, whatever is shown of it must be the real thing and
legible at viewing size. A brief glimpse of an unreadable screen adds nothing and takes time the
piece does not have.

## Text at the close
A closing line carries the message the viewer should leave with. It needs contrast against whatever
is behind it and enough time on screen to be read once comfortably, which is longer than it appears
when reviewing on a large display.

## Before finishing
Add up what the duration actually has to contain, and confirm nothing required has been quietly
left out.
