# Craft context

## Pace is a decision with consequences
Cutting speed is not a surface style; it changes what a viewer can absorb. Fast cutting creates
energy and urgency and reduces how much of each image is understood. Slower cutting allows detail
and mood to register and risks losing attention. In a very short piece this choice cannot be
hedged, because there is not enough time for the piece to be both.

## When a brief asks for both
A request for energy and calm at once is a real conflict and should be surfaced as one. There are
honest resolutions: let the pace change across the piece, so it opens calm and accelerates or the
reverse; or hold the cutting slow and create energy inside the frame through movement instead.
What does not work is cutting at a medium rate and hoping it reads as both.

## Every cut needs a reason
A cut should deliver something the previous shot could not — new information, a new emotional
beat, a change of scale. Cuts made only to keep things moving read as restlessness. In a very
short piece each additional cut spends a meaningful fraction of the total duration, so the number
of shots is itself a budget.

## Sound and picture share one attention
Music, voiceover and effects compete with the image for the same limited attention. When a
voiceover carries the main message, the image should support it rather than demand independent
attention at the same moment. Dense visuals under a dense voiceover mean neither is absorbed.
Silence, or a reduction in sound, is a legitimate way to make something visual land.

## Product presentation inside motion
If a product must be recognised, at least one shot needs to hold long enough for it to be read,
and the product should be lit and framed so its form and label are clear. Rapid cutting across a
product that has never been established leaves a viewer with an impression but no recognition.

## Endings
A short commercial usually ends with the one thing the viewer should retain. Whatever that is —
a name, an offer, an action — it needs enough time on screen to be read at viewing size.

## Establishing before moving
A viewer needs to know what they are looking at before they can appreciate how it is presented. In a
short piece this means at least one early shot that clearly establishes the subject, held long enough
to be read. Opening on an extreme close-up or a fast-moving detail is visually appealing and
frequently leaves the audience a second behind for the rest of the piece.

## Music and its role
Music sets expectation faster than image does, and a viewer forms a judgement about what kind of
piece they are watching within the first moments of hearing it. Choosing music that matches the
intended register, rather than music that is simply energetic, does much of the tonal work. Where a
voiceover carries the message, the music must leave room for it.

## Consistency of the product across shots
If a product appears in several shots it must look like the same object each time — the same colour,
the same packaging, the same proportions. Viewers notice inconsistency in a familiar object
immediately, and it undermines confidence in everything else the piece claims.

## Working within the duration
Before planning shots, divide the available seconds among the beats the piece must contain. This
usually reveals that the brief contains more than the time allows, which is far better discovered at
the planning stage than during assembly.

## Before finishing
Check the number of shots against the duration, and confirm the main message is legible.
