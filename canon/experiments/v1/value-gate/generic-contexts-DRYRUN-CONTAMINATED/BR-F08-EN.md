# Craft context

## Two people in conversation
A two-person exchange has to establish who is speaking, who they are speaking to, and how they
relate — all quickly. Consistent eyelines are what make this work: each person should appear to
be looking toward where the other actually is. If both appear to look the same way, they read as
looking at a third thing rather than at each other.

## Cutting between speakers
Cuts in dialogue are usually motivated by speech, but cutting exactly on each line makes an
exchange feel mechanical. Letting a cut land slightly before or after a line begins — so that a
reaction is seen, or a line starts over the previous shot — makes the exchange feel observed
rather than staged. Sound running continuously across the cut helps hold the moment together.

## Consistency across shots
Both people must remain recognisably themselves across every shot: same faces, same clothing,
same positions relative to each other and to the room. Where a brief specifies a shot structure,
that structure is a requirement, and the sequence should be checked as a whole rather than shot
by shot.

## Emotional register in difficult categories
Subjects involving risk, health or money can be approached through fear or through reassurance.
Fear gains attention and frequently produces avoidance rather than action. Where a brief
explicitly asks for warmth, that is a constraint on the whole approach, not a note about colour:
the situation should be recognisable and ordinary rather than alarming, and the exchange should
end somewhere the viewer can act from.

## Naming the real problem
People act on how a situation makes them feel more than on its technical description. An
exchange that surfaces an unspoken worry — uncertainty, not being sure, having assumed something
— tends to land harder than one that states a fact about a product.

## Closing
Where a piece ends on an instruction, that instruction should be simple, specific and achievable
immediately, and should hold on screen long enough to be read.

## Setting and its implications
The environment in which a conversation takes place carries meaning about the relationship between
the people in it and about the seriousness of the subject. A domestic setting suggests an ordinary
moment rather than a formal one, which is usually appropriate where the aim is to make a difficult
subject approachable.

## Performance and naturalism
Dialogue that reads well on the page can sound stilted when performed. Short exchanges, incomplete
sentences and pauses tend to sound more like real speech than complete, balanced lines. Where a brief
supplies exact dialogue, it should be delivered as written, but the surrounding performance can still
be relaxed.

## Framing a two-person exchange
Matching the framing of both people — the same shot size, the same height, the same distance — treats
them as equals in the conversation. Deliberately mismatching it implies a power difference, which
should be a choice rather than an accident of how the shots were composed.

## Duration and the number of beats
A twenty-second piece containing two lines of dialogue and a closing card has room for the exchange
to breathe, but not for additional business. Adding establishing material or extra action compresses
the dialogue and undermines the moment the piece exists to create.

## Sound
Speech clarity is the priority. Room tone should be continuous across cuts so the exchange feels like
one moment, and music should not compete with the dialogue.

## Before finishing
Check that both people stay consistent, that the eyelines match across every cut, and that the
tone requested by the client is actually what the piece delivers.
