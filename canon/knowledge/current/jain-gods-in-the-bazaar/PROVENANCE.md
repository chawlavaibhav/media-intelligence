## What kind of evidence this book contains

This is the **only source in the CANON-014 batch with first-hand field evidence of its own**, and
getting its bounds right is the most important thing in this file.

**What it has.** Ethnographic fieldwork carried out 1994–2001, with short follow-ups to 2001 and
building on earlier work from 1991–92: interviews with named calendar artists, printers, publishers,
agents and consumers, and first-hand observation of the industry's structure and functioning in
Sivakasi, Delhi, Mumbai, Nathdwara, Chennai, Madurai and elsewhere. The author names her informants —
Yogendra Rastogi, Indra Sharma, B. G. Sharma, Ram Kumar Sharma, J. P. Singhal, S. S. Shaikh, Venkatesh
and Maya Sapar, S. Murugakani, S. Courtallam, Ram Waeerkar and others — and thanks them individually.
She was trained as a visual communication designer at India's National Institute of Design and worked
as a graphic designer before entering the academy, and the granularity of the production detail
reflects it: she describes the retouching stage, the plate counts, the file assembly, the
letterpress overprinting and the agent's markup at a level of specificity no secondary source would
have.

**What it does not have.** No experiment, no survey, no reception study, no audience measurement, and
no quantitative work of any kind on how viewers respond to these images. Every claim in this book
about what a viewer does or believes arrives at one remove — as something a practitioner said about
their customers.

**The author states this bound herself, and it is extracted as an object.** At the head of the chapter
that rests most heavily on interviews she writes that these accounts are "not so much ... transparent,
consistent truths but ... narratives, produced largely for my benefit," and that they are subject to
reading and interpretation "as with any 'text'." She also identifies her own self-introduction as a
fine-art researcher as the probable trigger for a whole rhetorical register in her informants'
responses. This is captured as `sk_jgb_0180` and grouped as `scs_jgb_002` with the objects that
depend on it, so that a downstream consumer cannot retrieve the frontal-gaze constraint or the
muscularity injunction without also retrieving the author's own statement about what that evidence
is.

**One further bound, from the book's last page.** Jain closes by denying that images are powerful in
themselves: "It is we ... who make them powerful through what we do with them and what we say about
them." That is captured as `sk_jgb_0170`. Any downstream use that treats one of this book's
production constraints as a lever on an outcome is contradicted by the book's own conclusion.

## Where the evidence is unusually strong

Three of this source's production constraints are claims about **what an artefact looks like**, and
are therefore settleable by looking. They were looked at. The visual pass (documented in full in
`visual-evidence-ledger.yaml`) rendered seven figures and confirmed four separate
production-constraint claims and two compositional readings against the artefacts the text cites:

- the retouched print is visibly more saturated and higher in contrast than the original painting it
  was made from (fig. 88 photographs the two side by side, which makes it a controlled comparison);
- the "front-looking" imperative is visible in fig. 92, where a woman watching television has her face
  turned frontally out of the frame and watches the screen from the corner of her eye;
- the injunction against divine musculature is visible in fig. 93, where Ram and Lakshman draw bows in
  battle with half-lidded eyes and smooth unmuscled bodies;
- the muscular Ram of the late 1980s (fig. 138) differs from that depiction exactly as claimed, and
  the muscular-Hanuman-above-unmuscled-Ram contrast (fig. 140) is present within a single print.

**This is a real strengthening and it is precisely bounded.** Inspection establishes that the
constraints are real and visible. It establishes nothing about why they exist, nothing about whether
the practitioners' reasons for them are true, and nothing about what any viewer does with such an
image.

## Where the evidence is weakest

Three objects are flagged here because their weakness is easy to miss behind the book's confidence:

1. **`sk_jgb_0160`, the muscular Ram.** The shift is well attested and accepted from another scholar
   (Anuradha Kapur). The *explanation* — that Hanuman was the route by which muscularity entered the
   vocabulary — is Jain's own counter-proposal, advanced in chapter 7, which she introduces as "an
   experiment" whose narrative "unapologetically index[es] a certain will to power." It is the least
   evidentially secure object in the record and says so.
2. **`sk_jgb_0150`, the post-liberalisation characterisation.** Written from observation to roughly
   2004–06 and published in 2007. It predates the Indian smartphone and streaming eras entirely and
   carries a severe freshness risk, recorded on the object.
3. **`sk_jgb_0110` and `sk_jgb_0120`**, on meta-popularity and on critique as performative, are
   theoretical positions illustrated by cases, not measured effects, and are typed
   `source_interpretation` accordingly.

## Sensitive material

Chapters 6 and 7 deal with Hindu nationalism, the Ramjanmabhumi movement, communal violence, the 1996
case against M. F. Husain, and a 1987 sati. **This material is extracted as iconographic and
political history and carries no implication about any community.** No binding in this record touches
religious practice as such: what is bound is the general shape of a constraint — a convention that
outlives its cause, an enabling condition mistaken for a grading criterion, a catalogue mistaken for
a demand curve — never devotional content. The author's own position, that condemnation of an image
can shore up the power it condemns, is recorded as `sk_jgb_0120` with the note that it is an argued
position and not a demonstrated result.

The source also records the labour conditions underlying Sivakasi's cost position, including child
labour in the match industry and the absence of occupational health and safety across all three of
the town's major industries in the late 1990s. That is carried in the caveats of `sk_jgb_0050`
rather than dropped, because the mechanism being described has this cost and the source names it.

## Overlap with live Canon

**No work overlap, no author overlap and no publisher overlap** with any of the 19 accepted live Canon
sources, or with the three sources already extracted on this branch (Desai, Parameswaran, Pandey).

There is a **real and directional relation with the other book added in this pass**, and it is
recorded rather than buried:

- Dwyer and Patel, *Cinema India* (2002), **cite Kajri Jain by name** in chapter 2, note 9 — "Kajri
  Jain, 'Gods in the Bazaar', *South Asia*, XXI/1 (1998)". That is the article this book grew out of.
  The citation runs **from** Dwyer/Patel **to** Jain, five years before this book appeared; this book
  does not cite Dwyer and Patel.
- The two share a substantial body of third sources: Christopher Pinney on chromolithography and
  *Camera Indica*, Geeta Kapur on frontality and iconicity, Ashish Rajadhyaksha on Phalke, Tapati
  Guha-Thakurta, Patricia Uberoi, Partha Chatterjee.
- Both treat the same nineteenth-century origin material — Ravi Varma, chromolithography, the Bombay
  School of Art — and both discuss the artist S. M. Pandit.

This is **not** treated as destroying independence, and it is **not** treated as licensing a merged
concept. It is recorded pairwise with the direction of citation named, in
`canon/findings/CANON-014-CANDIDATE-LINEAGE-MATRIX.yaml`. No `cross_source_concept` was created. The
terms the two books share — frontality, iconicity — are held as source-local terms in each record,
with each source's own gloss, because the two authors do not use them identically: Dwyer reports
Kapur's category and applies it to cinema, while Jain treats frontality as a functional requirement of
ritual use.

There is also a **third-party relation worth the Controller's attention**: Jain's Sivakasi material and
Parameswaran's advertising material (`parameswaran-nawabs-nudes-noodles`, already on this branch)
both describe Indian commercial visual culture of the same decades from opposite ends — one from the
vernacular print trade, one from the English-medium agency world. Jain's chapter 1 argues that these
two arenas were institutionally separate, which makes the pair complementary rather than
corroborating. That observation is recorded in the lineage matrix and no promotion follows from it.
