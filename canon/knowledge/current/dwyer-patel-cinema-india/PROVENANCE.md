**The publisher discrepancy is recorded and is not resolved from this copy.** The filename says
Rutgers University Press; the copyright page says Reaktion Books, twice, with a London address and a
UK ISBN. Reaktion titles in this period commonly had separate North American distribution, and a
Rutgers edition is the obvious explanation, but **this copy contains no evidence for it** — there is
no Rutgers imprint, no US ISBN and no US copyright line anywhere in the scan. What is established is
that the copy in hand is the Reaktion setting. The filename is recorded as a fact about the file and
is not treated as bibliographic evidence. This is the same class of finding as the 2015/2016 date
discrepancy already recorded for `pandey-pandeymonium` on this branch, and is handled the same way:
the book's own imprint page governs.

The work's identity is not in doubt: title page, running heads, series page, contents and the
authors' Preface all agree.

## Representation integrity

**This is a scan, and it is a scan of a particular library's copy.** The PDF's Creator string is
`Internet Archive (Scribe Version 4.5-initial-80-gce32ee1e)` and its 732 image objects fall in the
Internet Archive's characteristic per-page pattern. The scanned copy belonged to Chester College, a
college of the University of Liverpool: its stamps, accession number `01090206` and class mark are
visible on the scanned pages and OCR into the text layer as noise in the front matter. These are
facts about the physical copy that was scanned; no extracted claim rests on any page carrying them.

**The OCR layer is reliable for prose and captions and unreliable for display type.** This was
checked rather than assumed. Running prose extracts cleanly and in order across all 244 pages.
Failures are concentrated where one would expect them — stylised and old-style lettering — and they
fail *loudly* rather than silently: the authors' names in the title-page display font render as
`Ree ie VW Ver Ren dally VAMP AT EL`, `DKNY` renders as `pkNny`, `1990s` renders as `r990s`, and
running folios render as `IO`, `IRL`, `T§`, `ly`. Garbage of this kind cannot be mistaken for
content, which is what makes it recoverable. **No printed page number in this record was taken from
the OCR of a folio** — every one comes from the mechanically established mapping below.

A duplicate-sentence scan over the whole extracted text for sentences above 90 characters returned
**zero** duplicates. There is therefore no pull-quote flattening and no reading-order damage in this
copy, unlike the `pandey-pandeymonium` copy on this branch where the same scan found ten.

## The division of labour between the two authors

The book states its own division in the Preface and this extraction respects it. Dwyer, a film
scholar, wrote chapters 1 and 2 (history of Indian cinema; film style — settings and costume). Patel,
a curator at the Victoria and Albert Museum, wrote chapters 3 and 4 (the history of film advertising;
how posters communicate meaning). The Introduction and Conclusion are joint. **Every object in
`source-knowledge.yaml` is attributed to the chapter it comes from, and therefore to the right
author.** This matters at one point specifically: both authors use the term *darshan*, but Patel
explicitly downgrades hers to a "secular darshan," so the two are not asserting the same thing, and
the record does not merge them.

## The evidence this book contains, and what it does not

This is an academic book by two specialists and it is careful about its own claims. What it contains
is **readings of artefacts** — posters, booklets, handbills, stills, sets, costumes — by people
trained to read them, supported by the scholarly literature on Indian cinema and Indian visual
culture (Prasad, Vasudevan, Rajadhyaksha, Geeta Kapur, Pinney, Guha-Thakurta, Dyer, Bourdieu,
Brooks) and by interviews with practitioners (Yash Chopra, Diwakar Karkare, D. G. Pradhan, Rajesh
Vaidya of Balkrishna Arts, Sharmishta Roy).

What it contains **nowhere** is a measurement. There is no audience study, no survey, no viewing
experiment, no eye-tracking, no box-office analysis of the authors' own, and no count over any
corpus. Where the text says a device "conveys", "engages" or "triggers", that is a trained reading of
an artefact, not an observation of a viewer. **Sixteen of the eighteen objects in this record are
therefore about conventions and their readings, and none carries `empirical_within_source`.** The
two exceptions are not exceptions of measurement but of documentation: `sk_dpci_0180` records a
campaign timeline with print-run and spot-count figures for one named 2000 release, and
`sk_dpci_0170` records named firms and dates for the shift to computer composition. Neither is a
measured effect; both are reported facts about an industry, with the source's own attribution and no
corroboration.

The single most important consequence: **`sk_dpci_0090`, on the attention-drawing power of the human
face, must not be cited as evidence for that effect.** It is a 2002 art-historical assertion resting
on developmental and expressive premises. Its value to this project is the boundary it draws between
a putatively general effect and a culture-bound one, not the effect it posits.

## The source dates itself

The Conclusion states that the 'filmi' style the book documents "is a thing of the past and is
considered to be the very antithesis of the new 'look'." This is captured as `sk_dpci_0190` and is
grouped with the other bounding statements as `scs_dpci_002` precisely so that a downstream consumer
cannot retrieve one of this book's conventions without also retrieving its expiry. Chapter 3's whole
argument is a periodisation; the Introduction declares a survival bias in the evidence. **Nothing in
this source supports a standing description of "the Indian film poster" or "Indian film style," and
the book says so itself.**

## Visual evidence

A **partial** first-hand pass was run and is fully documented in `visual-evidence-ledger.yaml`. Eleven
of approximately 121 numbered illustrations were rendered and read directly; the other 110 were not.
Seven distinct visual claims by Patel were checked against the plates they describe and **all seven
hold** — including the split colouring of Bachchan's face in the *Deewaar* poster, the knife-laid
overpainting over a photographic base, and the composition of the 1980s *Mother India* redesign.

The pass was bounded by cost, not blocked: the copy is complete, the page mapping is established, and
any further plate can be rendered on demand. That distinction is recorded in the ledger because it
changes what the partiality means for the audit.

## Overlap with live Canon

**No work overlap, no author overlap and no publisher overlap** with any of the 19 accepted live Canon
sources, or with the three sources already extracted on this branch (Desai, Parameswaran, Pandey).

There is, however, a **real and load-bearing relation with the other book being added in this pass**,
and it is not a coincidence of subject:

- Dwyer and Patel **cite Kajri Jain by name** — "Kajri Jain, 'Gods in the Bazaar', *South Asia*,
  XXI/1 (1998)" — in chapter 2, note 9. That is a citation of the article from which
  `jain-gods-in-the-bazaar` grew into a book five years later.
- The two books share a substantial intellectual milieu: both draw on Christopher Pinney's
  *Camera Indica* and his work on chromolithography, on Geeta Kapur on frontality and iconicity, on
  Ashish Rajadhyaksha on Phalke, on Tapati Guha-Thakurta, and on Patricia Uberoi.
- Both treat the same nineteenth-century origin material — Ravi Varma, chromolithography, the Bombay
  School of Art — and both discuss the artist S. M. Pandit.

This is **not** treated here as destroying independence, and it is **not** treated as licensing a
merged concept. It is recorded pairwise, with the direction of citation and the shared third sources
named, in `canon/findings/CANON-014-CANDIDATE-LINEAGE-MATRIX.yaml`, where the Controller can see it.
No `cross_source_concept` was created, and the overlapping terms (frontality, iconicity) are held as
source-local terms in each record.
